## What is it

Two live templates for writing a job down so a team can build against it. The **job story** format — "When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [expected outcome]" — was popularised by Alan Klement with Intercom in 2013. Ulwick's **outcome-driven job statement** is a verb, an object of control and a contextual clarifier. Jobs theory explains why either works; this is about the sentence.

## Purpose

The statement is what survives the research. Transcripts get archived; the sentence travels into briefs and backlogs, read by people who never met a participant. Name a solution in it and the design space closes before anyone opens it; leave out the situation and nobody knows when the need fires.

## When to use

- You have interview evidence and need a durable form of the need
- You are replacing persona-shaped user stories — "as a busy manager, I want…" — with something anchored in circumstance, not identity
- You need the needs stated so they can be scored and ranked, which is why Ulwick's format exists

## Structure

- **Job story — situation first.** "When an important new customer signs up, I want to be notified, so I can start a conversation with them." No persona, no feature: how the notification arrives is a design decision, not the job.
- **Outcome-driven statement — measurable.** Verb, object, clarifier, no adjectives: "get breakfast while commuting to work." Its desired-outcome statements add a metric and a direction — "minimize the time it takes to identify the correct drill bit size" — so each can be rated for importance and satisfaction.
- **The two disagree; don't blend them.** The job story is a narrative about progress that resists decomposition; Ulwick's is a functional job broken into measurables. Mixed, you get a list nobody can empathise with or survey.

## How to use

- Take the situation clause from a transcript. A real trigger — "the invoice bounced back a second time" — is what makes it actionable.
- Write the motivation as progress, not interface: "I want to know the payment cleared", not "I want a green badge".
- Test stability: if it reads as nonsense against the technology of fifteen years ago, it names a solution. A job outlives whatever serves it.
- Make the outcome clause say what the motivation makes possible, not the motivation again.

## Example

A freelance invoicing tool's backlog read "as a freelancer, I want payment reminders so I can get paid faster." From the transcripts: "When a client is two weeks past due and I still need them next month, I want to chase the invoice without sounding aggressive, so I can be paid without losing the client." That last clause moved the team from a bigger *Send reminder* button to editable reminder tone.

## Common mistakes

- **A user story with new punctuation** — "When I log in, I want to see my dashboard, so I can see my dashboard." The outcome restates the motivation and the situation is a screen, not a circumstance.
- **A solution in the motivation** — "I want to filter by tag" is a feature; the job is whatever finding the right thing quickly serves.
- **Adjectives in the job statement** — "quickly", "easily", "reliably" belong in the desired outcomes, not the job.

## Limitations

- **It formats evidence; it produces none.** A job story written in a workshop is a well-formed guess, and both templates are fluent enough that it sails through review.
- **Neither format prioritises.** Ulwick's ranks needs only after a separate survey; job stories do not rank at all.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Redrafting persona-shaped user stories as job stories, flagging any with no situation to draw on
- Checking a batch for named solutions, adjectives, and restated outcomes

**Where it fails**
- Inventing the situation clause. It can only come from something that happened to somebody; a model supplies a fluent, average trigger no participant described — a statement that reads correct and points nowhere.
