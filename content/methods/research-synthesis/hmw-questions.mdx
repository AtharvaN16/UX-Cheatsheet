## What is it

The question form that turns a framed problem into an invitation to generate. Min Basadur coined it at Procter & Gamble in the early 1970s, shortening Sid Parnes' "in what ways might we"; IDEO and the Google Ventures sprint carried it into design practice. Each word works: *how* presumes a solution exists, *might* keeps it provisional so a bad idea costs nothing, *we* makes it the room's problem rather than one person's brief.

## Purpose

The bridge between synthesis and ideation. A POV or insight statement is a claim about a person; an HMW converts it into something a group can answer without re-litigating the problem. Get the question right and ideation is cheap; get it wrong and no facilitation rescues the session.

## When to use

- You have a framed problem — an insight, a POV, an opportunity area — and need to open it up
- At the start of a divergent block, so brainstorming, crazy-8s and scamper all aim at one target
- When a team keeps arguing about solutions because nobody wrote down the question they are answering

## How to do it

- Restate one framed need as a question beginning "how might we", keeping the person and the situation in it.
- Read it for scope: the workable band is a question you could answer five genuinely different ways. Too broad — "how might we reinvent banking?" — generates nothing usable; too narrow contains its own answer.
- Re-scope a bad one with the standard moves: amplify the good, remove the bad, explore the opposite, question an assumption, go after the adjectives, change a status quo.
- Write several HMWs per insight and run two or three, rather than defending the first phrasing.
- Turn negatives positive. "How might we stop people abandoning checkout" invites removal; Basadur's point was that a positive framing invites design.

## Example

A scooter-share team's synthesis: first-time riders drop out at the helmet-law screen because they own no helmet and do not want to be told they are breaking the law. "How might we improve onboarding?" is too broad to sketch against. Rescoped: "how might we make a first ride feel safe and legal for someone with no helmet?" — answerable by dock-side helmet vending, a partner discount, or a slower, self-declared first ride.

## Common mistakes

- **A solution inside the question** — "how might we add a chatbot to cut support tickets?" The room now brainstorms chatbots; the session's scope was set by whoever wrote the sticky note.
- **Framing the business's problem, not the user's** — "how might we increase conversion?" is a metric, and ideas aimed at metrics drift toward dark patterns.
- **Separating the question from its evidence** — an HMW with no insight beside it drifts within a week into whatever the team already wanted.

## Limitations

- **Generation is not the deliverable.** A session that produces a hundred notes and no decision has produced nothing; the value is realised at selection, not generation.
- **It inherits the quality of the framing.** Built on a weak insight, an HMW launders it into a confident-sounding question a room will answer enthusiastically.
- **It cannot say whether the problem is worth solving** — that is prioritisation work, and an HMW makes any problem sound tractable.

## Tips

Keep each HMW physically next to the insight it came from and read both aloud before generating. Divorced from its evidence, a question gets judged on how interesting it sounds rather than on whose problem it solves.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Generating twenty phrasings of one HMW at different scopes, so a team picks a band rather than defends a first draft
- Screening a batch before a workshop for embedded solutions, metric-shaped framings and negative phrasings

**Where it fails**
- Writing HMWs from a product description instead of from synthesis. They come back sounding like a design brief, aimed at problems nobody in the research had — an HMW is worth exactly what the insight beneath it is worth.
