## What is it

A chronological visualisation of one actor's path toward one goal, built from research. Stages run across the top; lanes beneath carry what the person does, thinks and feels, which touchpoints they hit, and where it hurts. The standard skeleton, from Nielsen Norman Group: actor, scenario, phases, actions and emotions, opportunities.

Two boundaries. An **experience map** drops your product from the frame entirely; a journey map assumes your organisation is already in it. And neither is a flow — a flow is a route through an interface, a journey is an experience over time, including the stretches where your product is closed.

## Purpose

Turns scattered findings into one artefact a cross-functional room can argue with. The work is as political as analytical: the customer's sequence laid beside your org chart shows that the worst moment sits in the gap between two teams who each believe they own half of it.

## When to use

- After research, when teams have each read a different report
- When a quantitative anomaly needs a narrative — a churn spike tells you where, not what happened
- When ownership of stages is contested and the map is how you settle it

## How to do it

- Fix the actor and scenario in one sentence first. Two actors means two maps.
- Set stages from what participants did, not from your funnel; funnel stages import your model and hide everything between them.
- Source every cell to a quote, an observation or a log event. An unsourced cell is an assumption; mark it as one on the artefact.
- Plot emotion from what people said and showed — the curve is read first and invented most often.
- Validate against a fresh sample, then give every pain point one owner and a decision date. That last step is what separates a map from a poster.

## Example

A B2B analytics tool mapped "an ops lead brings their team onto a paid plan". The low point sat at data import, not pricing: a failed upload reported success, and the user found out days later, mid-presentation. Nobody owned it — the importer was Platform's, the confirmation copy Growth's.

## Common mistakes

- **Building it in a workshop from what the team already believes** — the result is a diagram of the room's assumptions, indistinguishable at a glance from a researched one. That resemblance is the danger: it gets cited for years.
- **Averaging several personas into one journey** — a composite path belongs to nobody, and its curve smooths away the peaks you needed.
- **Starting the map at your product** — journeys begin before awareness and continue after churn; cropping to your touchpoints redraws your assumptions.
- **Treating the render as the deliverable** — the insight is in the synthesis; outsource the making and you keep the poster, not the argument.

## Limitations

- **One actor, one scenario.** The scoping that makes it readable makes it silent about everyone else; a real customer base needs several maps that contradict each other.
- **Emotion is interpreted, not measured.** Two researchers draw the curve differently from the same transcripts, and the artefact shows no sign of it.
- **It goes stale silently.** Nothing in a map breaks when the product ships, so an old journey keeps looking authoritative.
- **It says nothing about delivery.** The staff and backstage systems behind each touchpoint need a service blueprint.

## Tips

Print the curve unlabelled and ask a stakeholder to narrate it. If they can't, the stages are wrong or the curve carries no evidence.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Clustering coded excerpts into candidate stages for a researcher to rearrange against the transcripts
- Auditing a finished map for cells with no linked evidence — the pass teams skip

**Where it fails**
- Generating the map from a product description. It returns the conventional discover-try-buy-use arc with a plausible emotion curve — exactly the assumption-based map the method exists to replace, and fluent enough to be harder to challenge than a colleague's guess.
