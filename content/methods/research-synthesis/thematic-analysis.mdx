## What is it

A systematic method for developing patterns of meaning across a qualitative dataset. Braun and Clarke's 2006 paper sets out six phases: familiarising yourself with the data, generating initial codes, searching for themes, reviewing themes, defining and naming themes, producing the report. It is the literature-backed version of what most teams do informally after interviews.

## Purpose

To get from a pile of transcripts to a few claims you can defend — including to the person who asks which extracts support theme two, and why you read them that way.

## When to use

- You need patterns across interviews, diary entries or open-text responses, not a per-participant summary
- The finding will be challenged or funded against, so the route from data to claim has to be inspectable

## How to do it

- **Familiarise.** Read everything once without coding. Braun and Clarke advise writing from this phase, not at the end.
- **Code.** Label segments across the whole corpus, coding for as many patterns as you can and keeping enough context to stay interpretable.
- **Generate initial themes.** Cluster codes into candidate patterns of shared meaning. The 2006 paper calls this "searching for themes"; the later work renames it *generating*, on purpose.
- **Review.** Check each candidate against its own extracts, then the whole dataset; what does not survive is split, merged or dropped.
- **Define and name.** Write a few sentences per theme saying what it is and is not. A theme you cannot define in prose is a topic.
- **Write up.** Build the argument around the themes, using extracts as evidence rather than decoration.

## Example

A team on payroll software codes 14 bookkeeper interviews. Codes like *checks the total twice*, *screenshots before submitting* and *keeps a shadow spreadsheet* collapse into one theme: bookkeepers maintain private verification because the system cannot show what changed. *Asks a colleague to re-check* sits under a second theme: approval is social, not a product state. A candidate called *frustration with the UI* is dropped in review: it is a topic the guide asked about, with no shared meaning across participants.

## Common mistakes

- **Writing "three themes emerged".** Braun and Clarke argue against exactly this phrasing: it is a passive account that denies the researcher's active role in identifying patterns, selecting which are of interest and reporting them; if nobody decided, nobody can be questioned.
- **Mistaking a topic for a theme.** "Onboarding" is where data lives; a theme is a pattern of meaning that cuts across topics.
- **Mixing variants.** Borrowing reflexive TA's language while demanding inter-rater agreement imports a standard that approach rejects.
- **Reporting counts.** "Nine of 14 participants" reads as measurement from a sample never built to support it.

## Limitations

- **It is not reproducible in the way the word implies.** Two competent analysts produce different theme sets from one corpus, and reflexive TA treats that as a property of interpretation, not a defect to measure away.
- **The label says nothing about standards.** Coding-reliability TA treats coder agreement as evidence of quality; reflexive TA rejects it. "We ran thematic analysis" leaves the reader guessing which rules applied.
- **Sample size has no formula.** Braun and Clarke's "(Mis)conceptualising themes" argues directly against sample-size tools for TA; you cannot pre-commit to a number.
- **It describes patterns, not mechanisms.** It will not tell you why a pattern holds or what would change it.

## Tips

Write the theme definition before you settle the name. Names attract agreement in a readout; a definition is what lets a colleague say "that extract does not belong here" — the only way a theme improves.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- First-cycle coding at volume, giving a candidate code list you edit against the transcripts
- Hunting disconfirming extracts for a theme you have defined, slow work by hand

**Where it fails**
- Generating the themes. Asked for themes, a model returns themes: fluent, shaped by the question rather than the corpus, and phrased in the passive register — "three themes emerged" — the literature was written to stop.
