## What is it

A model of how people accomplish something in the world, with no product and no organisation in the frame: stages, behaviours, needs and channels for a generic person pursuing a goal. Adaptive Path's 2013 guide calls it an archetypal journey aggregated from everyone going A to B.

Nielsen Norman Group's framing is the useful one: an experience map is the behaviour model you build *before* your product is in the picture, and a **journey map** is the product-specific one you build after.

## Purpose

Tells you where you fit instead of assuming it. Mapping how people close their books end to end exposes the moments they solve with a spreadsheet, a phone call or a friend — where an entrant has something to offer. Scoped to your product, those moments never appear.

## When to use

- You're entering a category you don't serve and don't know which part to build for
- Your product covers one slice of a wider behaviour, and support themes keep pointing at the slices you don't see
- Teams hold conflicting theories of what users do, none scoped past their own feature

## How to do it

- State the behaviour as a human goal with no vendor in it — "get a sick child seen today", not "book in our app".
- Recruit for range, not for your customers — people who chose a competitor, or nothing, hold what your users can't.
- Use methods that reach outside a session; diary studies and ethnography catch the waiting and the abandoning that interviews compress.
- Build stages from observed behaviour, then attach what the person wants, does and uses at each — workarounds, not features.
- Mark every moment served by something improvised: those are the candidate openings.
- Give each opening a named owner and a decision, or it becomes wall art.

## Example

A payroll startup for small restaurants mapped "a new hire's first paid shift" with no software in frame: a text asking someone to cover Friday, a handwritten rota, an ID photographed into WhatsApp, a payslip reconciled from memory. Payroll touched only the last stage; the unrecorded verbal swaps were where pay disputes started.

## Common mistakes

- **Running it as a workshop instead of research** — with no product in the frame, a map of the room's beliefs has nothing to contradict it: a diagram of the team's assumptions that reads exactly like a researched one.
- **Letting your product back into the frame** — name a stage after one of your screens and you have a journey map answering a different question.
- **Sampling only your own users** — they self-selected into the slice the map exists to look past.
- **Generalising to vacuity** — "becomes aware, considers, decides" fits everything and directs nothing. If the stages carry no behaviour specific to this domain, drop the map.

## Limitations

- **It doesn't tell you what to build.** It shows openings; which one you can serve profitably is a different question.
- **Expensive, and expensive early.** The breadth that gives it value means recruiting beyond your customers, when funding is thinnest.
- **Generalisation is a judgement with no test.** Aggregating several people into one path discards variation, and the artefact records nothing of what went.
- **It ages with the behaviour, not the product.** Slower — but a map made before a channel shift stays confidently wrong for years, with no symptom.

## Tips

Read the stage names and check no vendor, product or feature appears in any. One is enough to pull the map back toward your own model.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Drafting a screener that reaches non-adopters and competitor users, not just your own base
- Sorting mixed field material — diaries, forum threads, support logs — into candidate stages checked back against the notes

**Where it fails**
- Mapping a domain it has only read about. It produces a smooth, complete path assembled from published accounts of how the behaviour is meant to work, omitting the improvised workarounds the map exists to find.
