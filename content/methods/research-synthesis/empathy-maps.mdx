## What is it

A four-quadrant canvas — says, thinks, does, feels — filled in for one person from the record of a session you ran with them. Dave Gray designed it at XPLANE and published it in *Gamestorming* (2010).

## Purpose

Gets one person's account out of a transcript and onto a surface a team can read in thirty seconds, and makes the contradictions visible — the supervisor who says the scanner is fine and then re-counts every pallet by hand. The juxtaposition between quadrants is the payload.

## When to use

- Right after interviews or contextual inquiry, for a shared read on individuals before you synthesise across them
- As the input to a POV or insight statement about one person, where the specificity is the point

## Structure

- **Says** — verbatim quotes, in the participant's words
- **Does** — actions and behaviours you observed
- **Thinks** — beliefs you infer from hesitation, tone, word choice
- **Feels** — emotions you infer from the same clues

Two of those are observations and two are inferences — the d.school's instructions are explicit that thoughts and feelings cannot be observed directly and must be inferred from clues. The canvas shows none of that. Four identical boxes give a recorded quote and a guess about someone's inner life the same standing, which is how assumptions get cited back as findings a fortnight later.

## How to use

- Work one session at a time, transcript open beside the canvas.
- Fill *says* and *does* first, and only from the record. If you can't point at the line it came from, it doesn't belong in that half.
- Then fill *thinks* and *feels*, marking every entry — different ink, a bracket, an "I". That convention is most of what separates a useful map from a projection exercise.
- Pull needs and insights out at the edges, from tensions between quadrants rather than from one sticky note — and where an inference would change a decision, rewrite it as a question for the next session.

## Example

A team maps supervisor P2 after a warehouse visit. Says: "the scanner's fine, I just double-check." Does: re-counts every pallet against a paper sheet. Thinks, flagged inferred: expects to be blamed for a miscount. Feels, flagged inferred: quietly exposed. The tension across the seam is the finding — and because that half is flagged, the next visit asks about accountability rather than shipping reassurance on a guess.

## Common mistakes

- **Building the map with no research in the room.** A canvas assembled from what the team imagines is a map of the team's projections, formatted to look like evidence. With no session behind it, call it an assumption map.
- **Averaging several people onto one canvas.** NN/g separates one-user maps from aggregated ones; aggregating smooths away the variation that made the sessions worth running.
- **Leaving inferences unmarked.** Within a week nobody remembers which stickies were quotes, and the map gets cited as data.
- **Paraphrasing the says quadrant.** The participant's own words are the reusable asset; they feed labels, copy, and survey wording later.

## Limitations

- The name oversells it. Sorting quotes into four boxes is a synthesis exercise, not empathy, and can be done competently by someone who was never in the room.
- It captures one flattened moment. Sequence, duration, and the way a feeling turns mid-task fall out of the format.
- Nothing in the canvas tests an inference. A confidently wrong *thinks* entry propagates into personas and POV statements unchecked.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Extracting candidate *says* and *does* entries from a transcript with timestamps, so each stays traceable to a line
- Surfacing contradictions between quadrants across a set of finished maps

**Where it fails**
- Filling in *thinks* and *feels*. Asked to infer inner life from a transcript, a model returns plausible emotional attribution with no access to the pause, tone, or glance a real inference rests on — and it never marks its output as inference. The map's existing failure mode, automated.
