## What is it

A persona drafted in a couple of hours from what the team already believes about its users, with no new research, and marked as such. Gothelf's version comes from a workshop where people write down their assumptions about the audience, then defend them to each other.

## Purpose

Two jobs. Surfacing disagreement that would otherwise stay implicit — three people can hold three different audiences in their heads for a year without noticing. And turning those beliefs into claims specific enough to test, which is what a research plan needs as input. A way of not starting with an unexamined audience, not a way of skipping research.

## When to use

- Before research, when a decision is being made and there is no data yet
- On a lean team that would otherwise skip personas and design for nobody in particular
- When "we know our users" has to become specific enough to be wrong — Gothelf's original use was executive alignment

## Structure

The same fields as a researched persona — behaviour, goals, context, vocabulary — plus two that carry the method:

- **The label.** "Assumptions, not research", with the date and who was in the room — on the artefact, in the filename, on the slide.
- **The claims.** Each belief phrased so it can turn out false: not "busy professional" but "checks the rota on a phone between visits, three times a day".

The label is load-bearing. Strip it and a proto-persona is indistinguishable from a researched one — the format is identical — so it gets treated as one, cited in a roadmap review six months later by people who cannot tell which kind it was.

## How to use

- Two to three hours, whole team, including whoever holds the strongest opinion
- Draft individually before comparing; the divergence between drafts is the real output
- Consolidate to three to five, keeping disagreements visible rather than averaging them away
- Rank the claims by risk and confidence — most load-bearing, least known — and hand that list to the research plan
- Set an expiry: after research, a revised proto-persona becomes a persona and an unrevised one is deleted

## Example

A team building an internal expenses tool runs a two-hour session. Two engineers assume submissions happen monthly at a desk; the finance lead assumes receipts photographed in taxis. Both go into a file labelled "ASSUMPTIONS, not research — March". Eight interviews later the taxi version holds for field staff, the desk one for office staff, and the tool has to serve both.

## Common mistakes

- **Dropping the label once it gets prettied up** — the Figma pass is what converts an assumption into an apparent finding.
- **Treating the workshop as the research** — "we did personas" lands in a status update and validation never gets scheduled.
- **Averaging the room** — one consensus proto-persona hides the disagreement that was the reason to run it.
- **Adding a stock photo and demographics** — looks researched, adds nothing testable.

## Limitations

- **It records team belief, not users**, and inherits every bias in the room, including who got invited. NN/g's warning: proto-personas are often inaccurate and can reinforce incorrect beliefs — a wrong assumption made concrete and memorable is harder to dislodge.
- **The label decays faster than the artefact.** Screenshot, paste, reuse — provenance is the first thing lost, and cannot be recovered from the file.
- **No coverage claim whatsoever.** Chapman and Milham's objections to personas all apply here, minus the data.
- **It cannot settle what it surfaces.** It shows the room disagrees; only research says who was right.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Widening the candidate set — audiences and situations nobody in the room thought to name
- Rewriting each belief as a falsifiable claim with a way to test it

**Where it fails**
- Writing the proto-persona itself. The value is a room disagreeing out loud about who the user is; a generated one arrives as confident, uncontested prose indistinguishable from a researched persona — the exact failure the label prevents.
