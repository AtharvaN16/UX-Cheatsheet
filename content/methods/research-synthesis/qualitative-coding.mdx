## What is it

Assigning a short label to a segment of data so it can be retrieved, compared and grouped later. Coding is the operation, not a method in itself: thematic analysis, grounded theory and the sticky notes on an affinity wall all rest on it, and their claims are made or lost here. Saldaña is blunt: it is an interpretive act, not data entry. Naming a segment is already a judgement.

## Purpose

To make a corpus navigable in terms you chose. Once 400 extracts carry 30 codes, you can ask what else happened around *abandons task and asks a colleague* — a question the transcripts cannot answer.

## When to use

- The corpus is too large to hold in your head and will be revisited later
- Several people are analysing the same material and their decisions have to be comparable
- You need to point at evidence for a specific claim months after the sessions

## How to do it

- Decide where codes come from. **Inductive (open)** codes are written from the data as you read; **deductive (a priori)** codes come from a research question or framework. Most work runs both, keeping a channel open for what the frame did not anticipate.
- Pick a grain and hold it. A code on a whole transcript is a filing label; a code on every clause gives 400 codes and no structure.
- Do **first-cycle** coding across the whole corpus before judging anything — Saldaña's term for the initial pass that stays close to the data.
- Then **second-cycle** coding: merge, split and reorganise into a smaller structured set. Categories appear here; it is analytic work, not tidying.
- Build the codebook as you go. Each entry needs a name, a definition, a boundary saying what does *not* belong, and one example extract.
- Recode the first transcripts at the end; the codebook you finished with is not the one you started with.

## Example

A team studying a delivery-driver app codes 22 diary entries. *Route deviation* and *customer contact* come from the research questions; coding then surfaces something unplanned — drivers photographing the parcel *and* the doorway, coded *self-protective documentation*. Segment boundaries decide the meaning: that line alone reads as a habit, but with the sentence after it ("in case they say it never arrived") it is dispute anticipation, the claim the team can act on.

## Common mistakes

- **Codes that name topics rather than meanings.** *Pricing* tells you where in the transcript you are; *compares price against a competitor mid-task* tells you what happened.
- **A codebook of names with no definitions.** Two coders diverge invisibly, and nobody can reconstruct which reading applied where.
- **Coding only what the frame predicted.** A purely deductive pass returns your own hypotheses, well-evidenced.
- **Treating code frequency as a finding.** Counts reflect how much people talked, which reflects your discussion guide.

## Limitations

- **Consistency looks like rigour.** A code applied faithfully across 40 transcripts can still be the wrong lens throughout, and consistency hides it.
- **Coding fragments narrative.** Segmenting loses sequence, so a coded corpus supports pattern claims better than process claims.
- **Second-cycle collapse is a judgement.** No procedure tells you when two codes are one, and that call shapes every theme above it.
- **It cannot reach what was not recorded.** Silences, tone and what a participant chose not to say leave no segment to label.

## Tips

Keep an explicit *unsure* code rather than forcing a decision, and note why each contested extract went where it did. Those notes, not the code list, let you defend the analysis.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Applying a well-defined codebook across a large corpus for a first pass you then spot-check
- Proposing merges among 200 first-cycle codes, as candidates for the second-cycle decision

**Where it fails**
- Building the codebook. Code names are where interpretation lives, and a model returns a neat taxonomy of the topics you asked about, with the exclusion boundaries — the part that makes coding checkable — quietly invented.
