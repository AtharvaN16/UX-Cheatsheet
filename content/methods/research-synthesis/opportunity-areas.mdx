## What is it

The named regions of the user's problem space where a team has decided it is worth working — stated as unmet needs, not things to build. They sit between synthesis and solutioning: after you know what is true, before you commit to what to make.

## Purpose

Where to work and what to build are two decisions, and collapsing them throws away the option space. "Make the fee structure predictable before checkout" admits a calculator, a simpler fee model, or an earlier total. "Add a fee calculator" admits one.

## When to use

- Synthesis has produced insights and somebody is about to turn them into tickets
- Several features compete for one slot and you need to know whether they address the same need
- A quarter is being planned and the honest input is four places worth working, not a backlog

## How to do it

- Start from insights, not ideas — each area should trace to evidence the friction is real.
- Write it in words a user could recognise, at the grain they feel it. "Checkout" is a screen; "I never know what I will actually pay until the last step, so I stop" is a moment with a cause.
- State what would have to be different for the area to count as addressed, in user terms. That is what stops it sliding back into a feature.
- Size and compare them with an existing prioritisation method rather than inventing a score: an impact/effort matrix for a fast read, RICE or ICE when the ranking must be defended, the Kano model for the kind of satisfaction on offer.
- For the rigorous form — an outcome at the root, a branching opportunity space, solutions and assumption tests below — graduate to an opportunity solution tree; a flat list is the lightweight version.

## Example

A payments team synthesising abandoned baskets names three areas: the final total is unpredictable; it is unclear which saved card gets charged; people who leave have no route back. The team arrived wanting a fee calculator — which addresses only the first, and now competes with showing the total earlier and with dropping the fee.

## Common mistakes

- **Writing a feature and calling it an area** — "notifications" is a solution with the problem sanded off: no visible evidence, and no way to tell what would count as solving it.
- **Naming one area and moving on** — the value is comparative. One area is a decision already taken, presented as a discovery.
- **Areas nobody can trace to research** — an unevidenced area is prioritised with the same confidence as the evidenced ones beside it.
- **Sizing by how loud the complaint was** — frequency in your sample is not frequency in your population.

## Limitations

- **This is where solutions get laundered.** The commonest real use of the phrase is retrofitting problem-space language onto something the team already wanted to build — a document that looks like discovery and functions as justification. The tell: only one solution ever fits the area.
- **Granularity is arbitrary and consequential.** The same evidence supports one broad area or six narrow ones, and that choice pre-decides how the work gets scoped and staffed.
- **An area carries no value of its own.** That somewhere is worth working says nothing about its worth against the others; that needs a prioritisation method and a business case the areas cannot supply.

## Tips

Ask whether a competitor could address this area in a way you would not have thought of. If not, you wrote your own solution in problem-space grammar.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Rewriting feature-shaped statements into problem-space phrasing, then flagging those that still admit one solution
- Checking a set for overlap, and for insights that no area covers

**Where it fails**
- Proposing the areas themselves from a product description or a persona. The output is a tidy list of the industry's generic problems, and reads as more complete than research-derived areas precisely because nothing in it is specific enough to be missing.
