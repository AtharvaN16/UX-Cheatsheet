## What is it

Solving a problem backward: instead of asking how to achieve a goal, ask what would guarantee its failure — then avoid those things.

## Why it matters

Some problems resist being solved forward. "How do I make this launch succeed?" is vague enough to invite optimism. "What would make this launch fail?" is specific enough to invite honesty. Munger's own framing — "I want to know where I'm going to die, so I'll never go there" — is the whole method in one line: avoiding stupidity is a more reliable route to a good outcome than chasing brilliance.

## Key ideas

- **Forward and backward are different questions**: "how do I succeed" and "what guarantees failure" don't always surface the same list — inversion catches blind spots the forward question skips.
- **It surfaces hidden assumptions**: asking what has to be true for a plan to fail forces you to name what has to be true for it to work, which is often left implicit.
- **Avoiding stupidity beats seeking brilliance**: a decision-maker who eliminates every obvious way to fail ends up with a shorter, higher-quality list of options than one chasing the single best move.

## Application

- Before committing to a roadmap, ask "what would make this the wrong roadmap in twelve months?" instead of only "why is this the right roadmap?"
- When a design pattern feels obviously right, invert it: "what would make this pattern a bad choice for this specific user and context?" — if the answer is easy to generate, the pattern isn't as safe as it felt.
- Use it as a cheap first pass before a formal pre-mortem: inversion is the individual mental habit; pre-mortem is the structured team exercise built on the same logic.

## Limitations

- **Generates a list, not a decision**: inversion tells you what to avoid; it doesn't rank or resolve trade-offs between the things worth avoiding.
- **Easy to do superficially**: a shallow "what could go wrong" list feels like inversion but misses the specific, concrete failure modes that make the technique useful — see pre-mortem's "prospective hindsight" for why specificity matters.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Inversion is just pessimism"**: it isn't advocacy for a negative outlook — it's a reasoning direction. The goal is still a good outcome; inversion is one more angle to find the assumptions that threaten it.
- **"Inversion replaces forward planning"**: it's a complement, not a substitute. Good decision-making runs both directions and compares what each one surfaces.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Generating a first-pass list of failure conditions for a stated plan, as raw material for a human to prioritize and verify against real context.
- Stress-testing a decision by asking a model to argue the inverted case explicitly, surfacing assumptions the original framing didn't examine.

**Where it fails**
- A model's inverted list tends toward generic, plausible-sounding failure modes rather than the specific mechanisms of *this* plan, in *this* market, with *this* team — the value of inversion comes from concrete specificity, and a fluent-but-generic list can look thorough while missing the actual risk.
