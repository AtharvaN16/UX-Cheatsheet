## What is it

Before committing to a decision, the team assumes it's one year later and the project has already failed — then generates specific, concrete reasons why. This "prospective hindsight" — imagining the failure as already real — produces far more honest risk assessments than asking "what could go wrong?"

## Purpose

Most planning discussions suppress dissent — the person with doubts about a plan is reluctant to speak up mid-planning, especially once the group has converged on enthusiasm. A pre-mortem exists specifically to make dissent safe and structured: everyone is asked to imagine failure, so raising a concern isn't a personal objection to the plan, it's simply doing the assigned exercise. It's cheap to run and disproportionately valuable for important, hard-to-reverse decisions.

## When to use

- Before committing to a decision that's expensive or hard to reverse — a launch, an architecture choice, a hire, a major redesign.
- When a team has converged quickly on a plan and you suspect enthusiasm, not scrutiny, is driving the consensus.
- As a lower-cost alternative or complement to red-teaming, when you want the whole team's honest doubts rather than one adversarial reviewer's case.

## How to do it

1. State the plan clearly, as if it were already final.
2. Ask the team: "It's one year from now. This project failed. Write down, individually and silently, the specific reasons why." Prospective hindsight — treating the failure as an already-real fact, not a hypothetical — is what makes people specific instead of vague.
3. Collect every reason individually before discussing as a group, for the same reason heuristic evaluation collects findings independently first: open discussion collapses the range of concerns people are willing to name.
4. Go around and have each person read their top reasons aloud; capture every one, without judging or debating yet.
5. Group similar reasons, then rank by how likely and how damaging each failure mode seems given current evidence.
6. For the highest-priority failure modes, decide now what to change about the plan to reduce their probability — this step is the actual payoff; a pre-mortem that stops at a list of fears has done half the exercise.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating it as a one-time formality** — running it after the decision is effectively already locked in produces the appearance of scrutiny without the ability to act on what's found.
- **Discussing findings before collecting them individually** — group discussion before individual writing reintroduces the exact social pressure the technique is designed to bypass.
- **Stopping at the list of failure reasons** — the exercise is only useful if it changes the plan; a pre-mortem with no follow-up action is a documented list of ignored warnings.
- **Running it only with people invested in the plan succeeding** — including someone with less stake in the outcome tends to surface concerns insiders won't voice.

## Tips

Make it genuinely safe to name reasons that implicate the plan's leadership or its most enthusiastic advocates — a pre-mortem where the obvious failure modes go unsaid because they'd embarrass the project's champion isn't doing its job.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Generating an initial, broader list of plausible failure modes than a team might produce unprompted, as raw material for the team's own individual pre-mortem writing — not a replacement for it.
- Helping cluster and prioritize a long list of team-submitted failure reasons quickly once they've been collected.

**Where it fails**
- The technique's power comes specifically from a real team, with real stakes and real domain knowledge, confronting concrete failure — a model's imagined failure modes are generic pattern-matches from similar-sounding projects, not evidence grounded in this team's actual blind spots, and substituting them for the team's own reasoning defeats the purpose.
