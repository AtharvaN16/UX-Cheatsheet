## What is it

A prioritization formula that ranks backlog items by dividing each one's Cost of Delay by its job size: WSJF = Cost of Delay ÷ Job Duration. It sequences work to maximize economic outcome, not just to work through the biggest or loudest items first.

## Purpose

Answers "which job should we do first" with an economic argument instead of a political one: prefer whichever item returns the most value per unit of time spent building it. Reinertsen's underlying point — inherited directly into WSJF — is that most teams don't know the cost of delaying something, and without that number, sequencing decisions default to opinion.

## When to use

- Sequencing a portfolio or program backlog of epics/features that differ meaningfully in both value and size
- You're in or adjacent to a SAFe context where Cost of Delay and relative-sizing conventions already exist
- Two initiatives seem similarly valuable, but one is far smaller — WSJF is built specifically to catch the case where the smaller job should go first because it returns value faster
- You want a repeatable, defensible ranking rather than a one-off argument about priority

## Structure

- **Cost of Delay** = User-Business Value + Time Criticality + Risk Reduction/Opportunity Enablement, each scored on a relative scale (commonly a Fibonacci-like sequence: 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 20).
- **Job Duration** (or size) — the relative size of the job, scored on the same kind of scale.
- **WSJF score** = Cost of Delay ÷ Job Duration. Higher score, higher sequencing priority.

## How to use

1. Assemble the set of items to sequence (SAFe typically applies this at Epic, Capability, or Feature level within a defined backlog).
2. For each item, score User-Business Value, Time Criticality, and Risk Reduction/Opportunity Enablement relative to the others in the set — not in absolute terms.
3. Sum those three to get Cost of Delay.
4. Score relative Job Duration/size for each item, on the same comparative scale.
5. Divide Cost of Delay by Job Duration for each item to get its WSJF score.
6. Sequence highest score first. Re-run the exercise whenever the backlog composition changes meaningfully — the scores are relative to the current set, not absolute.

## Example

Two epics: Epic A has high business value and moderate time criticality but is large (size 13); Epic B has moderate value but is small and quick to ship (size 3). Even if Epic A scores higher on raw Cost of Delay, dividing by size can put Epic B ahead — it returns a smaller amount of value, but returns it much faster, which is exactly the case WSJF is designed to catch that a value-only ranking would miss.

## Common mistakes

- **Scoring in absolute terms**: WSJF's Fibonacci-like scales are meant for relative comparison within one set of items, not fixed units — mixing absolute dollar figures with relative story-point-style scores produces meaningless ratios.
- **Skipping Time Criticality and Risk Reduction**: teams default to scoring Cost of Delay as pure business value and ignore the other two components, which quietly biases the ranking toward big, obviously-valuable items over urgent or risk-reducing smaller ones.
- **Re-scoring too rarely**: because scores are relative to the current backlog, adding or removing items changes what the numbers mean — a stale WSJF ranking from three backlogs ago isn't just outdated, it's not comparable.
- **Using it below the level it's built for**: WSJF is meant for epics/features being sequenced against each other, not for ranking individual small tasks — Impact/Effort or MoSCoW fit that scale better.

## Limitations

- **Every input is a relative estimate, not a measurement**: the formula is only as good as the group's honesty and calibration when scoring value, criticality, risk, and size.
- **Assumes items are genuinely comparable**: ranking a compliance-mandated item against a growth bet on the same scale can produce a number that technically computes but doesn't reflect the real decision.
- **Adds process overhead**: the scoring ceremony has a real cost; for a small team or backlog, that overhead can exceed the value of the extra precision over a simpler matrix.

## Tips

Score Time Criticality and Risk Reduction/Opportunity Enablement explicitly and separately from Value, even though it's tempting to bundle everything into one "how important is this" number — the whole point of decomposing Cost of Delay is to catch urgency and risk that a pure value score would miss.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Drafting an initial relative-size estimate across a large epic list from written descriptions, as a starting point for the team's own estimation session
- Checking score consistency across a large backlog — flagging items scored similarly on inputs but wildly differently on final WSJF, which usually indicates a scoring error worth re-checking

**Where it fails**
- Generating the value, criticality, or risk scores themselves without the team's actual judgment behind them. WSJF's output is only meaningful because it reflects a real group's calibrated relative judgment about their specific backlog; a model's plausible-sounding scores for items it has no real stake in evaluating produce a ranking with the appearance of rigor and none of the actual signal.
