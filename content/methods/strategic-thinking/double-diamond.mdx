## What is it

Two diamonds in sequence, each widening then narrowing: **Discover** and **Define** make up the first (find and frame the right problem), **Develop** and **Deliver** the second (find and ship the right solution).

## When to use

- You're structuring a project end to end and need a shared vocabulary for "which phase are we in" across designers, PMs, and stakeholders who don't share a design background
- A team is jumping straight to solutions before the problem is actually understood — the shape forces a visible "Discover/Define" pass before anyone's allowed into "Develop"
- You need to justify, to a skeptical stakeholder, why the team is spending time on research and reframing instead of shipping
- You're onboarding a non-design team into a design process and need something simpler than a full design thinking curriculum

## Structure

1. **Discover** — go wide. Research the problem space: user research, market context, stakeholder interviews. No solutioneering yet.
2. **Define** — narrow. Synthesize Discover's findings into a specific, framed problem statement. This is where most teams skip too fast — reframing the problem is the actual value of the phase, not just summarizing research.
3. **Develop** — go wide again. Generate and iterate on multiple candidate solutions to the *defined* problem — sketches, prototypes, concepts.
4. **Deliver** — narrow again. Test, refine, and ship the strongest solution; the diamond ends with something in the real world, not a deck.

## How to use

Treat each diamond's midpoint (the transition from wide to narrow) as a real checkpoint, not a formality — write down the problem statement at the end of Define and the concept direction at the end of Develop, and get explicit sign-off before narrowing. Design Council's own updated Framework for Innovation adds four principles around the diamonds (be people-centred, communicate visually and inclusively, collaborate and co-create, iterate) because teams kept treating the original diagram as a rigid waterfall rather than a repeatable, overlapping loop — in practice, real projects loop back into Discover from Develop constantly.

## Example

A team is asked to "redesign the checkout flow." Discover surfaces that the actual problem isn't checkout friction — it's that users don't trust the shipping-cost calculation until the very last step. Define reframes the brief from "redesign checkout" to "surface shipping cost earlier and make it feel trustworthy." Develop explores several ways to do that (upfront estimator, cart-page breakdown, address-based live pricing); Deliver ships and measures the one that tested best.

## Common mistakes

- **Skipping straight to Develop** — treating the diamond as decoration on a process that was always going to start with a solution. If Discover and Define didn't change anything about the brief, they weren't done.
- **Treating it as strictly linear** — real projects loop backward (a Develop-stage prototype test can reopen Define). Presenting the diamond as a one-way pipeline sets up stakeholders to be confused when the team needs to go back.
- **Confusing the shape with a methodology** — the Double Diamond tells you *when* to diverge and converge, not *how*. Teams that stop there, without a concrete method for each phase (interviews for Discover, sketching for Develop, etc.), end up with an empty diagram.

## Limitations

- **No prescribed methods** — it says nothing about which research or ideation techniques to use inside each diamond; you still need Design Thinking, Lean UX, or a design sprint to actually do the work.
- **Implies more sequence than most real projects have** — mature product teams run continuous discovery and delivery in parallel, not as two discrete diamonds per project; the model maps awkwardly onto that reality.
- **Says nothing about business viability or feasibility trade-offs** explicitly — it's a design-process shape, not a full product framework.

## Variations

Design Council's own 2019 update, the Framework for Innovation, keeps the diamonds but wraps them in four working principles and explicitly allows for looping and overlap rather than a strict four-stage handoff — closer to how teams actually work in practice.

## Tips

Use the diamond as a project-status shorthand in stakeholder conversations ("we're still in Discover, we haven't defined the problem yet") more than as a rigid gate — its real value is giving non-designers a one-glance mental model for why the team isn't building yet.
