## What is it

A human-centered problem-solving mindset built around five modes: **Empathize**, **Define**, **Ideate**, **Prototype**, **Test**. Where Double Diamond gives the divergent/convergent shape of a project, Design Thinking is the specific exercises you run inside it.

## Purpose

Gets a team unstuck from solutioning based on internal assumptions by forcing direct contact with users before defining the problem, and forcing prototyping before committing resources to a solution. Brown's 2008 framing was explicitly about applying a designer's methods — not just designers themselves — to problems beyond product screens: services, business models, organizational change.

## When to use

- The problem is ambiguous or contested and the team's first instinct is to jump straight to a solution
- You need buy-in from non-designers on why more research and more prototyping cycles are worth the time
- The challenge spans more than an interface — a service, a process, an organizational problem — where "design" methods aren't the obvious first move but empathy and iteration still apply
- You're running a discrete workshop or project phase, not looking for an every-week operating cadence (that's Continuous Discovery)

## How to do it

1. **Empathize** — talk to and observe real users in context. Interviews, contextual inquiry, shadowing. Resist writing requirements yet.
2. **Define** — synthesize empathy findings into a specific point of view: a user, a need, an insight, usually framed as a "How Might We" question.
3. **Ideate** — generate a volume of possible solutions before evaluating any of them. Divergent by design; defer judgment.
4. **Prototype** — build the cheapest, fastest artifact that lets you test the idea — paper, clickable mockup, role-play, whatever's fastest.
5. **Test** — put the prototype in front of real users, capture what breaks, and loop back to Define or Ideate as needed. The modes are not a strict sequence — d.school explicitly frames them as modes you revisit, not phases you finish.

## Common mistakes

- **Running Ideate before real Empathize work** — brainstorming without user contact just produces confident guesses with a design-thinking label on them.
- **Treating the five modes as a rigid, one-way waterfall** — NN/g's multi-year practitioner research found teams that got the most value treated the modes as iterative and overlapping, not sequential; teams that ran it as a strict pipeline reported weaker results.
- **Conflating it with Double Diamond** — Design Thinking is Brown/d.school's specific named process with its own vocabulary (empathize, ideate); Double Diamond is a more generic shape that doesn't prescribe those specific exercises. Using the terms interchangeably confuses stakeholders who've read one but not the other.
- **Stopping at Prototype without ever testing with real users** — a prototype nobody outside the team has seen is just a more expensive assumption.

## Tips

Time-box Ideate hard — the point isn't the best idea in the room, it's enough volume that the team stops anchoring on the first plausible solution. NN/g's research also found the method's value comes less from any single tool and more from the underlying habits (talk to users, defer judgment, build to think) — teams that adopted the mindset outperformed teams that just ran the workshop format once and called it done.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Synthesizing raw empathy-interview transcripts into candidate "How Might We" statements for a human to vet against what users actually said
- Generating volume during Ideate — a fast way to get past the first three obvious ideas before the team's own divergent thinking kicks in

**Where it fails**
- Standing in for the Empathize phase itself. A model can summarize a transcript, but it cannot notice the hesitation in a user's voice, follow an unexpected tangent, or build the trust that makes a user share the real problem instead of the polite one — the empathy work has to come from a human being in the room.
