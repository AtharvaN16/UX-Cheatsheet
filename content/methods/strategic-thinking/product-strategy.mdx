## What is it

The small set of choices about who you serve, what problem you solve for them, and how you win — the thing that shapes the roadmap, what gets built, and what gets deliberately left undone. As a designer, you rarely author the document, but you design inside it.

## Purpose

Gives a team a shared answer to "why this and not that" so a hundred smaller decisions don't each need a meeting. A real strategy names a sacrifice — a market it won't chase, a feature it won't build — because a plan with no trade-offs isn't a strategy, it's a wish list.

## When to use

- Kicking off a new product area or major initiative, where the roadmap has no coherent "why" yet
- A roadmap conversation keeps re-litigating the same disagreement because there's no stated position to point back to
- You're a designer trying to figure out whether a request fits the actual strategy or is scope creep dressed as a priority
- Leadership alignment has drifted and decisions are increasingly being made ad hoc, initiative by initiative

## How to do it

1. Start from a real customer and problem, not a market category — "who exactly are we building for, and what are they stuck on" beats "we're building a platform for X industry."
2. State the choice about how you win, not just what you'll build. Helmer's 7 Powers framing is useful here: name the specific benefit (better margins, higher retention, lower cost) and the barrier that stops a competitor from copying it once it works — a strategy that's only a good idea, with no barrier, gets copied in a quarter.
3. Write down what you are explicitly not doing, and why. This is usually the hardest and most valuable sentence in the document.
4. Connect the strategy to a small number of measurable bets — not vague aspirations — so it's falsifiable rather than just aspirational.
5. Socialize it as a working document that changes as you learn, not a plaque. Rachitsky's "Strategy Blocks" framing treats strategy as decomposable, revisitable pieces rather than a single monolithic plan handed down once.
6. Push the strategy down to a level a designer can act on: it should be specific enough that you can tell whether a given feature request is on-strategy or off-strategy without asking.

## Common mistakes

- **A roadmap with no stated outcome**: a list of features isn't a strategy — it's the symptom sometimes called the build trap, optimizing for shipped output with no stated bet on what outcome it produces.
- **No sacrifice, so no strategy**: if the document doesn't name something the team is deliberately not doing, it's a wish list wearing a strategy's clothes.
- **Writing it once and never revisiting**: markets move; a strategy that isn't updated as assumptions get tested calcifies into stale direction nobody actually follows.
- **Strategy written for executives, illegible to designers**: if a designer can't use the document to decide whether a given feature belongs, it hasn't actually been pushed down to where decisions get made.
- **Confusing a Power with a good idea**: an improvement that's easy to copy isn't a moat, however good it is — the barrier matters as much as the benefit.

## Tips

Test your own strategy with the question a designer should always ask of any decision framework here: what would make this fail, and would you notice? A strategy nobody could ever prove wrong isn't specific enough to be useful.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Stress-testing a draft strategy as a skeptical stakeholder — generating counter-arguments and the most likely reasons a competitor could copy the approach
- Summarizing scattered strategy inputs (research notes, competitive scans, past roadmap docs) into a first-draft structure for a human to sharpen

**Where it fails**
- Generating the strategic choice itself. The hard part of product strategy is picking what to sacrifice under real uncertainty about your specific market and org — a model producing a fluent-sounding strategy document creates the appearance of judgment having happened without the underlying trade-off actually being made by anyone accountable for it.
