## What is it

A theory of causation for why customers buy: people don't buy products, they "hire" them to make progress on a specific job, and "fire" whatever they used before for whatever does the job better. The job stays stable even as the products competing to do it change completely.

## When to use

- You're trying to understand why a product is or isn't being adopted, and demographic/persona-based explanations keep coming up empty
- You're exploring a new market and need to find the actual job being poorly served today, rather than starting from a feature idea
- Growth has stalled and you suspect the product has drifted away from the job that originally got it hired
- You want the causal "why" underneath the "what" that discovery interviews or opportunity-solution-tree work surfaces

## Structure

- **The job**: the progress a customer is trying to make in a given circumstance — stated as a struggle, not a demographic ("get through a boring commute without getting hungry," not "male, 25-34, commuter").
- **The circumstance**: jobs are triggered by situation, not identity — the same person hires different things for the same underlying job depending on context (time pressure, who else is around, what's already failed).
- **Hiring and firing**: every purchase is a substitution decision against whatever was doing the job before, including "doing nothing" — competitors are defined by the job, not the product category.
- **Functional, social, and emotional dimensions**: the job usually has more than a purely functional component — how the customer wants to feel or be seen while getting it done matters as much as the mechanical outcome.

## How to use

1. Pick a recent purchase or switch — someone who just started using (or just abandoned) a product in your category — and interview them about that specific event, not their general opinions.
2. Reconstruct the timeline: what was happening right before they went looking for something, what alternatives they considered (including doing nothing), and what tipped the decision.
3. Separate the functional job from the social and emotional dimensions — ask what would have made them feel foolish or smart, not just what technically worked.
4. Write the job statement at the level of the struggle, deliberately excluding your product category, so it stays stable even if your product changes entirely.
5. Map current alternatives against the job, including non-obvious competitors (a whiteboard competing with project management software for "get everyone aligned in the room").
6. Use the job, not a persona, as the unit you design and prioritize against — features get evaluated by how much better they do the job versus the best current alternative.

## Example

Christensen's milkshake study found that most milkshakes were sold in the morning, to solo commuters who weren't buying anything else. The job wasn't "satisfy a craving for a milkshake" — it was "give me something to do with my one free hand that's filling, takes a long time to consume, and doesn't make a mess on the dashboard." That reframing changed the real competitive set from other desserts to bananas, bagels, and boredom, and pointed at a completely different set of product improvements (thicker shake, harder straw-resistance) than a taste-preference survey ever would have.

## Common mistakes

- **Writing the job as a feature request in disguise** — "the job is to have a search bar" is a solution wearing a job's clothes; the job is the underlying struggle the search bar would resolve.
- **Segmenting by demographics instead of circumstance** — two people with identical demographics can be hiring completely different things for completely different jobs; the job, not the persona, is the stable unit.
- **Only asking about the functional dimension** — ignoring the social and emotional job (wanting to look competent, avoid a fight, feel in control) misses why two functionally identical products succeed and fail.
- **Treating the theory as a replacement for evidence** — jobs theory is a lens for interpreting real interviews about real past behavior, not a brainstorming technique performed from an armchair.

## Limitations

- **Requires real interviews about real past events** — done as a desk exercise with imagined customers, it produces plausible-sounding job statements with no evidentiary grounding.
- **Doesn't prioritize for you** — it tells you what job to design for, not which of several underserved jobs is worth pursuing first; that's a separate strategic call.
- **Hard to falsify cleanly** — because job statements are somewhat interpretive, two researchers can read the same interview and write different job statements, which makes JTBD easy to do badly while feeling rigorous.

## Tips

Interview about the last time the switch actually happened, not about general habits or hypotheticals — "walk me through the last time you needed to do this" produces concrete, checkable detail; "how do you usually handle this?" produces a flattering summary.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Drafting candidate job statements from a batch of raw interview transcripts, as a starting point for a human to check against the actual quotes.
- Spotting patterns across many interviews — recurring triggering circumstances, recurring "fired" alternatives — that are tedious to hold in working memory across a long study.

**Where it fails**
- Generating job statements without underlying interview data. A model will happily produce a fluent, specific-sounding job statement ("the job is to feel in control during a stressful commute") for a product it has never seen real customers use — the theory's entire value is that the job statement is extracted from what actually happened in someone's life, and a model's guess, however well-phrased, isn't that.
