## What is it

A 2x2 matrix that plots candidate ideas by expected impact against required implementation effort, sorting them into quick wins, big bets, fill-ins, and money pits.

## Purpose

Gives a room a fast, visual way to separate "do this now" from "don't bother" without a scoring model — useful precisely because it's cheap to run and easy for a mixed group (design, product, eng) to fill in together from shared intuition.

## When to use

- Early triage of a long list of ideas, bugs, or improvement candidates, before investing time in a heavier scoring method
- A workshop or roadmap session where you need visible, fast alignment rather than a precise rank order
- You want a shared artifact that non-specialists can read at a glance — the quadrant names alone communicate the decision
- As a first pass before a smaller, higher-stakes shortlist gets a more rigorous method like RICE

## Structure

- **Quick wins** (high impact, low effort): do these first — the obvious priority.
- **Big bets** (high impact, high effort): worth pursuing, but sequence deliberately against capacity.
- **Fill-ins** (low impact, low effort): fine for slack time, never the main focus.
- **Money pits** (low impact, high effort): avoid — the quiet roadmap-killers if no one flags them.

NN/g's version of this is flexible by design: swap either axis for other criteria, or run multiple matrices, if impact/effort specifically isn't the right pair of dimensions for the decision at hand.

## How to use

1. List every candidate item.
2. Estimate impact: the expected user or business benefit if built, ideally anchored to a specific outcome rather than a gut "this feels important."
3. Estimate effort: rough implementation complexity and time, at a level of precision matched to how early-stage this triage actually is.
4. Plot each item on the grid and read off the quadrant.
5. Sequence quick wins first, negotiate big bets against actual roadmap capacity, and use the exercise itself as the argument for cutting money pits.

## Example

A support team's backlog of small product complaints: adding inline validation to a form that generates a large share of support tickets is a quick win (high impact, low effort); rebuilding the whole account settings flow is a big bet (high impact, high effort, worth scheduling deliberately); adding a rarely-used export format is a fill-in; rewriting a legacy integration used by almost no one is a money pit.

## Common mistakes

- **Skipping a real impact definition**: without anchoring impact to something specific (tickets reduced, conversion moved, retention affected), "impact" collapses into whoever argues most persuasively.
- **Effort estimated by one person's gut, not the team building it**: engineers systematically see effort differently than product or design; skipping their input skews placement.
- **Using it for a decision that deserves more rigor**: it's a fast triage tool, not a substitute for RICE or real data when the stakes or ambiguity are high enough to warrant one.
- **Never revisiting placement**: like any prioritization snapshot, both impact and effort estimates decay as you learn more — treat the matrix as disposable, not permanent.

## Limitations

- **Only two dimensions**: strategic fit, dependencies, and urgency don't appear on the grid at all.
- **Estimates are opinion, not measurement**: without real data behind either axis, this formalizes a group's current beliefs, not the truth.
- **Blurs into Cost vs Value**: "effort" here is closer to raw implementation work than the broader cost concept (including risk, maintenance) used in a Cost vs Value matrix, and "impact" is closer to user-facing benefit than the business-value framing Cost vs Value uses — don't treat the two matrices as interchangeable or the axes as meaning the same thing.

## Tips

Timebox the estimation step deliberately — the whole value of this method is speed; a team that spends an hour precisely calibrating "effort" has defeated the purpose and should have used a heavier method instead.
