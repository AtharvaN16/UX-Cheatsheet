## What is it

A framework for choosing large-scale, quantitative UX metrics along five categories — Happiness, Engagement, Adoption, Retention, Task success — paired with a Goals-Signals-Metrics process for deriving the actual numbers from a stated product goal, instead of grabbing whatever's easiest to instrument.

## When to use

- You need to report on UX quality with real numbers, at a scale where individual usability tests or interviews can't cover enough users
- Stakeholders keep asking for "the UX metric" and you need a structured way to pick one that isn't an arbitrary engagement number
- You're setting product goals and want metrics that are demonstrably tied to user experience, not just business output
- You need to track whether a redesign actually improved the experience over time, not just whether it shipped

## Structure

- **Happiness**: attitudinal — satisfaction, perceived ease of use, likelihood to recommend. Usually gathered via surveys (SEQ, NPS-style items), because it can't be inferred from behavior alone.
- **Engagement**: level, depth, or frequency of use — sessions per user, features used, time spent on a specific meaningful action (not just raw time-on-site).
- **Adoption**: how many new users start using a product or feature, in a given time period.
- **Retention**: how many existing users come back over time — the closest thing to a truth signal, since retention can't be gamed the way self-reported satisfaction can.
- **Task success**: efficiency, effectiveness, and error rate for specific tasks — completion rate, time on task, or error frequency, usually gathered from instrumented behavioral data rather than a lab study.

## How to use

1. Start from the product goal, not the metric — the paper's key insight is that HEART categories are a menu, not a checklist; most products don't need all five tracked at once.
2. Use the Goals-Signals-Metrics process: state the goal in plain language, list the signals that would indicate progress (things you could plausibly observe), then pick the specific metric that operationalizes each signal.
3. Assign each goal to the HEART category it actually belongs to — a "make onboarding smoother" goal is Task success, not Engagement, and picking the wrong category leads to tracking the wrong signal.
4. Instrument the metric and set a baseline before changing anything, so a later comparison is meaningful.
5. Revisit the mapping when the product goal changes — a metric that made sense for last quarter's goal can quietly become the wrong thing to optimize once priorities shift.

## Example

A team redesigning a checkout flow might state the goal as "reduce friction in checkout," derive the signal "fewer users abandon partway through," and land on the metric "cart-to-purchase completion rate" under Task success — rather than defaulting to a generic Engagement number like "time spent on the checkout page," which would actually reward a worse, slower checkout.

## Common mistakes

- **Picking a metric first and reverse-engineering the goal**: the paper's whole structure exists to prevent this — start from the goal, or you end up optimizing whatever was easiest to instrument.
- **Using Happiness data alone as "the UX metric"**: satisfaction surveys miss the behavioral truth serum that Retention provides; a product can score well on Happiness while quietly losing users.
- **Trying to track all five categories for every project**: HEART was explicitly designed as a menu you select from per-goal, not a mandatory five-part dashboard for every team.
- **Confusing Engagement with a business vanity metric**: raw session count or page views can rise while genuine experience quality falls — the framework asks for engagement metrics tied to meaningful use, not just volume.

## Limitations

- **Requires enough scale to be statistically meaningful**: it was built for products with large user bases generating continuous behavioral data; small products or early-stage prototypes don't have the volume to make HEART metrics stable.
- **Happiness still relies on self-report**: the one category that captures attitude, not behavior, inherits all the usual limitations of survey data (response bias, small sample sizes relative to the behavioral metrics).
- **Doesn't diagnose *why* a metric moved**: a dropping Task success rate tells you something broke, not what — it still needs qualitative methods (usability-testing, heuristic-evaluation) to explain the number.

## Tips

Write the Goals-Signals-Metrics table out loud with the team before touching an analytics tool — the discipline of naming the signal in plain language, separately from the metric, is what catches a mismatched category before it becomes a quarter of tracking the wrong thing.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Drafting candidate Goals-Signals-Metrics mappings from a written product goal, as a starting proposal for the team to argue over and refine.
- Summarizing large volumes of open-text Happiness survey responses into themes that complement the quantitative score.

**Where it fails**
- Selecting the final metric without a human confirming it can't be gamed. A model can produce a plausible signal-to-metric mapping that sounds rigorous, but whether a given metric actually reflects the stated goal in this specific product — versus something correlated but gameable — is a judgment call the paper explicitly treats as a team discussion, not a formula.
