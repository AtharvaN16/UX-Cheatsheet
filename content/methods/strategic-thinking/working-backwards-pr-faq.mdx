## What is it

Amazon's practice of writing a mock press release and FAQ for a product *before* building it, forcing the team to articulate the customer benefit in plain language before any roadmap or spec exists.

## Purpose

Kills weak ideas cheaply, in writing, before they cost engineering time. A press release forces specificity: if the "benefits" listed wouldn't actually excite a real customer reading them, that's a signal to rework or kill the idea now, while a redraft costs an afternoon — not after months of building. The FAQ half does the same for internal risk: it front-loads the hard questions (cost, feasibility, competitive response, legal) that would otherwise surface piecemeal during execution.

## When to use

- You're deciding whether a significant new product or feature is worth building at all — not refining something already greenlit
- Leadership keeps approving ideas based on decks and roadmap slides that never state a concrete, falsifiable customer benefit
- Multiple teams or stakeholders need to align on what's being built and why before any design or engineering work starts
- You want a written artifact that forces rigor through repeated drafting, not a single meeting's worth of discussion

## How to do it

1. Write the press release first, as if the product has already launched: headline and subheading naming the product and the specific customer it's for; a summary paragraph with the (hypothetical) launch date; a problem paragraph describing the customer pain point in their terms; a solution paragraph explaining how the product solves it and how it's meaningfully better than alternatives; and a quote plus a "getting started" line.
2. Write the FAQ in two parts: **external FAQs** anticipate what customers and press would ask (pricing, availability, how it works); **internal FAQs** anticipate what executives and stakeholders will ask (cost, technical risk, market size, why now).
3. Circulate the PR-FAQ for written or in-meeting feedback and revise — expect many drafts; the rigor comes from the redrafting, not the first version.
4. Use the document as the actual go/no-go artifact: if the press release doesn't sound genuinely exciting to a real customer, that's the signal to change the idea or kill it, not to polish the writing.
5. Once approved, keep the PR-FAQ as the north-star reference during build — teams periodically re-check "are we building what's in the press release?" and treat drift as something to explain, not ignore.

## Common mistakes

- **Writing the press release after the product is basically built** — at that point it's marketing copy, not a planning tool; the entire value is catching a weak idea before resources are committed.
- **Skipping the internal FAQ** — a press release alone reads well but hides the hard operational and financial questions; the internal FAQ is where those get forced into the open early.
- **Treating the first draft as final** — Amazon's own practice depends on brutal, repeated rewriting; a single draft rubber-stamped in one meeting defeats the purpose.
- **Writing benefits nobody would actually care about and shipping anyway** — the whole diagnostic value of the exercise is killing ideas that don't survive being stated plainly; overriding that signal because the roadmap slot is already committed defeats the method.

## Tips

Write the press release from the customer's vocabulary, not the company's — if a benefit only makes sense to someone who already knows the internal roadmap or tech stack, it will fail the "would a real customer find this exciting" test that makes the exercise useful in the first place.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Drafting the first pass of the FAQ's likely internal questions (cost, risk, competitive response) as a checklist for the actual owner to answer with real numbers
- Tightening press-release prose across redrafts once the substantive customer benefit and problem framing are already settled by a human

**Where it fails**
- Writing the problem and solution paragraphs from scratch without firsthand customer knowledge. A fluent, specific-sounding press release is exactly what this method uses as a go/no-go signal — a model can produce one that reads convincingly without any of the underlying customer insight, which defeats the entire diagnostic point of the exercise and risks green-lighting an idea on the strength of good prose alone.
