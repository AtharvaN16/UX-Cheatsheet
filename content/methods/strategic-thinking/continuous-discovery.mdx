## What is it

Weekly touch points with customers by the team actually building the product — small, continuous research activities in pursuit of a specific outcome, done every single week rather than in occasional research "sprints." It's a habit and an operating cadence, not a project phase with a start and end date.

## Purpose

Replaces the feast-or-famine pattern of research (a big study before a launch, then months of silence) with a steady weekly drip of customer contact that keeps assumptions from calcifying between check-ins. Torres's argument is that discovery done in occasional bursts always lags the decisions the team is already making — continuous discovery keeps evidence roughly as current as the decisions it's meant to inform.

## When to use

- A product team is making weekly roadmap and prioritization calls but the underlying user evidence is months old
- You have (or can form) a stable product trio that can commit to a recurring weekly cadence, not just a one-off research push
- The team needs an ongoing way to generate and test opportunities, not just validate one already-decided feature
- You're past the point of needing a single big study and need a sustainable rhythm instead

## How to do it

1. Define a clear, specific outcome for the team to organize discovery around (a business or product outcome, not a feature).
2. Stand up a recurring weekly slot for small customer research activities — interviews, prototype reactions, or informal chats — and protect it like any other standing meeting.
3. After each interview, log opportunities (unmet needs, pain points, desires) you hear directly from customers — not features you've already decided to build.
4. Build and maintain an opportunity solution tree: outcome at the root, opportunities as branches, candidate solutions and assumption tests underneath.
5. Update the tree every 3-4 interviews as new evidence comes in, rather than treating it as a one-time planning artifact.
6. Test the riskiest assumptions under the most promising solutions before committing engineering time, and feed results back into the tree.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating it as a research sprint that ends** — running weekly interviews for a month and then stopping defeats the purpose; the value compounds only if the cadence is sustained.
- **Logging features instead of opportunities** — writing down "add a filter" instead of the underlying unmet need ("I can't find last month's orders") collapses the opportunity space before the team's even explored it.
- **Skipping the outcome** — discovery without a defined outcome to organize around produces a pile of interesting but unprioritized findings with no way to decide what matters.
- **One person doing all the interviewing** — Torres's model is built around a full product trio engaging with customers, not a researcher who reports findings to a team that never talks to a user directly.

## Tips

Torres recommends at least 3-4 customer interviews before you have enough signal to meaningfully update the opportunity solution tree — don't restructure the whole tree off a single conversation, but don't wait for a large-N study either; the cadence itself is the point.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Synthesizing recurring themes across many weeks of interview notes into candidate opportunities for the tree
- Drafting interview guides tailored to the current outcome, for the trio to refine before each week's sessions

**Where it fails**
- Replacing the actual weekly conversation with customers. The habit's value is the team's own direct, repeated exposure to real customer language and behavior — a synthesis of past transcripts, however good, is not a substitute for the trio continuing to talk to people this week, and treating an AI summary as "discovery done" quietly ends the habit the method depends on.
