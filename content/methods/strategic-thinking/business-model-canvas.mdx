## What is it

A one-page, nine-block map of how a business creates, delivers, and captures value — who you serve, what you offer, how it reaches them, what it costs, and how it makes money.

## Purpose

Gets an entire business model onto one page so a team can see how the pieces connect and where they don't — a change to pricing or channel on one side of the canvas has consequences on the cost and customer-relationship side that a narrative business plan tends to bury in prose.

## When to use

- Designing a new product or business line and needing to check that the pieces (value, channel, revenue, cost) actually cohere before investing further
- Evaluating whether a proposed feature or pivot changes the business model in ways beyond the feature itself — new channel, new cost structure, new customer relationship
- Comparing two possible business models side by side rather than debating them in the abstract
- As a shared artifact across product, design, and business stakeholders who otherwise talk past each other using different vocabularies

## Structure

Nine blocks:

- **Customer Segments** — who you create value for.
- **Value Propositions** — what you offer each segment.
- **Channels** — how you reach and deliver to customers.
- **Customer Relationships** — the type of relationship each segment expects.
- **Revenue Streams** — how and from what the business makes money.
- **Key Resources** — the assets the model requires to work.
- **Key Activities** — the things you must do well for the model to work.
- **Key Partnerships** — the outside relationships that make the model work or make it cheaper/better.
- **Cost Structure** — the costs the model incurs to do all of the above.

## How to use

1. Start from Customer Segments and Value Propositions — get the core of who-and-what right before filling in the rest.
2. Work outward to Channels and Customer Relationships (how value reaches and is sustained with the customer), then Revenue Streams (how it's paid for).
3. Fill in the "engine room" — Key Resources, Key Activities, Key Partnerships, Cost Structure — the things required to actually deliver the value promised above.
4. Look for inconsistencies across blocks: a premium Value Proposition paired with a self-serve, no-touch Customer Relationship is often a mismatch worth interrogating, not a detail.
5. Use it comparatively — sketch the current model and a proposed alternative side by side rather than filling in one canvas in isolation.

## Example

A freemium analytics tool: Customer Segments are solo developers and small eng teams; the Value Proposition is fast setup with no sales call; Channels are self-serve web signup and developer community word-of-mouth; Revenue Streams are subscription tiers gated on usage volume; Key Resources are the hosted infrastructure and a lean support team; Key Partnerships include cloud infrastructure providers. Sketching this out surfaces, for instance, that a planned move to require a sales call for onboarding would directly contradict the low-touch Customer Relationship the rest of the model depends on.

## Common mistakes

- **Filling every box with confidence when most are actually assumptions**: the canvas doesn't distinguish validated fact from guess; treat unproven boxes as hypotheses to test, not settled decisions.
- **Doing it solo, design- or product-only**: Cost Structure and Revenue Streams need input from people who actually own those numbers, or the canvas becomes wishful.
- **Treating it as a one-time exercise**: a canvas filled in at kickoff and never revisited misses exactly the model breaks discovered once you're in market.
- **Using it for a feature decision it's too coarse for**: the canvas operates at the level of the whole business model; a single feature prioritization call belongs in RICE or Cost vs Value, not here.

## Limitations

- **No sense of relative priority within a block**: it tells you the shape of the model, not which Revenue Stream or Channel matters most.
- **Says nothing about competitive defensibility**: two companies can have near-identical canvases and very different outcomes depending on which one has an actual moat.
- **Assumes the model is roughly stable enough to draw**: for a genuinely emergent, pre-PMF idea, forcing all nine boxes can produce false confidence in a shape that hasn't been earned yet.

## Tips

Sketch the model as it exists today before sketching the proposed future state — comparing the two side by side makes visible exactly what would have to change, which is usually where the real risk and cost live.
