## What is it

A practice of applying Lean Startup's build-measure-learn loop and Agile's short cycles to UX work — treating every design as a falsifiable hypothesis instead of a polished deliverable. The unit of work shifts from "the mockup" to "the assumption being tested this cycle."

## Purpose

Fixes the mismatch between long, deliverable-heavy UX processes (personas decks, comprehensive wireframe sets, sign-off rounds) and agile engineering teams that ship every one to two weeks. Lean UX gives design a cadence and an artifact — the Lean UX Canvas — that fits inside that loop instead of running a parallel, slower track that becomes a bottleneck.

## When to use

- Design sits inside (or needs to sit inside) an agile/scrum team already shipping in short cycles, and the old deliverable-first process is the bottleneck
- The team keeps building based on unstated assumptions about users, business outcomes, or the market that nobody has written down or tested
- You need a lightweight way to align design, product, and engineering on what's actually being validated this sprint, not just what's being built
- Leadership is asking for less time spent on comprehensive documentation and more on validated learning per cycle

## How to do it

1. Fill in a Lean UX Canvas per initiative: business outcome, users, user outcomes, the assumptions underlying all of it, and the riskiest ones to test first.
2. State the riskiest assumptions explicitly as hypotheses: "We believe [outcome] will happen if [these users] have [this experience]. We'll know we're right when we see [signal]."
3. Design the smallest thing — a sketch, a prototype, sometimes real shipped code — that can produce evidence for or against the riskiest hypothesis, not the most complete version of the feature.
4. Build, ship, or test it fast enough to fit inside the team's existing sprint cadence.
5. Measure against the signal you defined upfront, not a vaguer "did it feel good" check.
6. Feed what you learned back into the canvas — update or discard the assumption — and repeat for the next-riskiest one.

## Common mistakes

- **Skipping straight to "make an MVP" without stating the hypothesis first** — an MVP without a defined assumption and success signal is just a smaller feature, not an experiment; you learn nothing you can act on.
- **Running it as a solo-designer practice instead of a team one** — Lean UX's whole premise is collapsing the handoff between design, product, and engineering; a designer quietly adopting the canvas alone doesn't get the collaboration benefit.
- **Treating "lean" as "less design"** — the method reduces upfront deliverables, not user research or design rigor; teams that use it as cover to skip research end up building fast in the wrong direction.
- **Never revisiting the canvas** — treating it as a one-time kickoff document rather than a living artifact updated every cycle as assumptions get validated or killed.

## Tips

Start with the outcome, not the feature — Gothelf and Seiden's core argument is that teams anchor too fast on a specific solution; forcing the canvas to open with a business and user outcome (not a feature name) keeps the riskiest-assumption exercise honest.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Drafting hypothesis statements ("We believe... we'll know we're right when...") from a rough feature idea, as a starting point for the team to sharpen
- Summarizing evidence across multiple small experiments into a single canvas update between cycles

**Where it fails**
- Generating the assumptions themselves without real user or usage data behind them. The entire method depends on ranking assumptions by actual risk to the business and actual uncertainty about users — a model asked to "list our riskiest assumptions" without that grounding will produce plausible-sounding guesses dressed up as prioritized risk.
