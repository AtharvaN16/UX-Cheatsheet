## What is it

A goal-setting system pairing a qualitative **Objective** (what you want to achieve, stated ambitiously) with a small set of quantitative **Key Results** (how you'll know you got there, stated as measurable outcomes, not tasks).

## When to use

- A team or company needs a shared, time-boxed way to state priorities and check progress — typically quarterly
- Leadership wants ambitious, stretch-level goals rather than safe, easily-hit targets
- You need to distinguish, in writing, between what you're trying to achieve (Objective) and how you'll know (Key Results) — teams that skip this distinction end up with a roadmap of outputs and no stated outcome
- You want a mechanism that forces prioritization: a small number of Key Results per Objective, not an exhaustive list of everything worth doing

## Structure

- **Objective**: a qualitative, ambitious, memorable statement of what you want to achieve — inspirational enough to motivate, specific enough to be unambiguous about direction.
- **Key Results**: 2-5 measurable outcomes that would prove the Objective was achieved — each one gradable with a number, not a "done/not done" checkbox.
- **Cadence**: OKRs are typically set and graded on a fixed rhythm (Google's default is quarterly, with some annual OKRs for longer-range direction).
- **Grading**: Google grades Key Results on a 0.0-1.0 scale rather than pass/fail, explicitly because stretch goals graded at 0.7 are often a better outcome than safe goals graded at 1.0.

## How to use

1. Write the Objective first, in plain language, answering "what do we want to achieve" — resist the urge to make it measurable; that's the Key Results' job.
2. Write 2-5 Key Results that would be true if, and only if, the Objective was actually achieved — if a Key Result could be hit while the Objective clearly wasn't, rewrite it.
3. Keep Key Results as outcomes, not tasks — "ship the new onboarding flow" is a task; "increase Day-7 activation from X% to Y%" is a Key Result. A shipped feature that doesn't move the number wasn't a completed Key Result.
4. Set OKRs at company, team, and (optionally) individual level, with lower levels supporting — not simply restating — the level above.
5. Track progress through the cadence, not just at the end — OKRs are meant to be revisited, not written once and rediscovered at the deadline.
6. Grade honestly at the end of the cycle, including partial and failed Key Results, and use the grading conversation to inform the next cycle's OKRs rather than treating a low score as a performance failure.

## Example

Intel's "Operation Crush" set the Objective of establishing the 8086 as the dominant 16-bit microprocessor, with Key Results that included specific benchmark wins and design-in commitments against Motorola — concrete, gradable outcomes that made it unambiguous, quarter by quarter, whether the company was actually winning the fight it had declared, rather than just staying busy on it.

## Common mistakes

- **Writing Key Results as a task list**: "launch feature X," "run 3 user interviews" are outputs with no outcome attached — a completed task list that doesn't move a number wasn't a successful Key Result.
- **Confusing OKRs with performance reviews**: Doerr and Grove both stress that stretch-goal grading (a deliberately ambitious 0.7) should not map directly to compensation or performance scoring, or people will quietly write safe OKRs to protect their score.
- **Setting too many Objectives at once**: an organization with a dozen "top" Objectives has no actual priority — OKRs' value comes from forcing a small, visible set of choices.
- **Skipping the mid-cycle check-in**: OKRs written in week one and revisited only at grading time become a compliance exercise rather than a working tool for prioritization.

## Limitations

- **Doesn't tell you which Objective is right**: OKRs are a structure for stating and tracking goals, not a method for deciding which goals are strategically correct — that judgment has to come from elsewhere (product-strategy, opportunity-solution-tree).
- **Cadence mismatch with discovery work**: quarterly Key Results can pressure teams toward committing to solutions before enough discovery has happened, especially when leadership expects a shippable Key Result by the deadline.
- **Easy to cascade badly**: OKRs that are simply copy-pasted or mechanically derived level by level down an org chart lose the local judgment about what actually matters at each level.

## Tips

Separate "committed" OKRs (must-hit, usually operational) from "aspirational" OKRs (stretch, expected to land around 70%) explicitly, the way Google does — grading both on the same 1.0-is-success scale quietly punishes ambition and rewards sandbagging.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Drafting candidate Key Results from a stated Objective and flagging which ones are actually tasks in disguise, for a team to argue over and tighten.
- Summarizing quarterly progress against Key Results into a plain-language update for stakeholders who don't need the raw dashboard.

**Where it fails**
- Grading whether an Objective was strategically the right one to set. A model can score whether Key Results are well-formed (measurable, outcome-shaped) with useful precision, but whether the underlying Objective represents the highest-leverage bet for the business this quarter is a judgment call about trade-offs and opportunity cost that the framework deliberately leaves to the humans setting it.
