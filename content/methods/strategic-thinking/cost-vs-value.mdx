## What is it

A 2x2 matrix that plots candidate work by estimated implementation cost against estimated business value, sorting everything into four quadrants to decide what to build first.

## Purpose

Gets a room to a prioritized shortlist fast, using estimates the team already roughly has, without building a scoring model. It's the blunt instrument you reach for before you've earned the right to something more rigorous like RICE.

## When to use

- Early-stage prioritization where you need a defensible first cut, not a precise rank
- The team has rough cost and value intuition but no hard usage or revenue data yet
- You need something a mixed room (design, eng, business) can fill in together in one sitting
- As a pre-filter before spending scoring effort on a smaller shortlist

## Structure

Two axes, four quadrants:

- **Low cost, high value**: build now — the obvious priority.
- **High cost, high value**: strategic bets — worth it, but sequence and resource deliberately.
- **Low cost, low value**: fill-ins — fine for downtime, never the main focus.
- **High cost, low value**: avoid — the items that quietly drain a roadmap if no one says no to them.

## How to use

1. List every candidate initiative.
2. Score cost: development time, technical complexity, risk, and downstream maintenance burden — not just "how many days."
3. Score value: how much it moves the business or customer outcome you actually care about, not how loudly someone is asking for it.
4. Plot each item and read off the quadrant.
5. Sequence low-cost/high-value first, then negotiate the high-cost/high-value bets against actual capacity.

## Example

A B2B SaaS backlog: a bug fix blocking checkout is low-cost/high-value (do now); a from-scratch permissions system is high-cost/high-value (roadmap it deliberately); a rarely-requested UI theme option is low-cost/low-value (fill-in); a bespoke integration for one customer is high-cost/low-value (say no).

## Common mistakes

- **Confusing cost with effort**: cost should include risk and maintenance burden, not just calendar days — a "quick" feature that's expensive to support long-term isn't actually low-cost.
- **Confusing value with loudness**: the customer who complains most isn't necessarily the one whose request moves the needle — value should trace to an actual business or user outcome.
- **Treating quadrant placement as objective**: this is a structured way to make a subjective conversation visible, not a measurement. Don't defend a placement as if it were data.
- **Never revisiting placement**: cost and value estimates decay as you learn more; a matrix filled in once at kickoff and never touched again is a stale artifact, not a decision tool.

## Limitations

- **Two dimensions hide everything else**: strategic fit, dependencies, and timing don't show up on the grid at all.
- **Estimates are opinion, not evidence**: without user research or usage data behind the value axis, you're prioritizing by whoever's most persuasive in the room.
- **Doesn't distinguish itself cleanly from Impact/Effort**: cost is not quite effort (cost includes long-term maintenance and risk; effort is closer to raw implementation work), and value is not quite impact (value is estimated business return; impact in an Impact/Effort matrix is usually framed around user-facing benefit) — pick one framing and use its definitions consistently rather than blending the two matrices.

## Tips

Write down what "cost" and "value" mean for this specific exercise before anyone starts plotting — the matrix is only as useful as the shared definitions behind the two axes, and most disagreements about placement are actually disagreements about definitions.
