## What is it

A visual structure connecting a single desired outcome at the root to a branching space of customer opportunities, then candidate solutions beneath each opportunity, then assumption tests beneath each solution. Laying discovery out this way makes visible how much opportunity space a team never explored.

## When to use

- A team keeps generating solutions ("add a search bar," "build a dashboard") without having named the underlying opportunity those solutions would address
- You're running continuous discovery — weekly customer touchpoints — and need a living structure to organize what you're learning, rather than a one-off research report that goes stale
- Multiple solutions are competing for the same slot on a roadmap and you need to see whether they actually address the same opportunity or different ones
- You want to make a team's assumptions about a solution explicit and testable before committing engineering time to build it

## Structure

- **Outcome (root)**: a measurable change in customer or business behavior — never a solution. This is usually the metric a north-star-metric or a specific OKR is meant to move.
- **Opportunities**: unmet customer needs, pain points, or desires that, if addressed, would drive the outcome — stated as problems, sourced from real interviews, never as a feature.
- **Solutions**: candidate products, features, or changes that could address a given opportunity — multiple solutions can and should compete under the same opportunity branch.
- **Assumption tests**: the smallest experiments that could show a belief underneath a solution is false, prioritized toward whatever is simultaneously most load-bearing and least evidenced.

## How to use

1. Write the outcome at the root in outcome language, not solution language — "increase weekly active users of feature X" is a stated outcome; "build feature X" already skipped the tree.
2. Run continuous customer interviews (weekly, with a cross-functional trio, per Torres's Continuous Discovery Habits) and add newly surfaced opportunities to the tree as branches under the outcome, rather than treating research as a one-time phase.
3. Resist collapsing straight from outcome to a single opportunity — deliberately map several plausible opportunities before picking which to pursue, so the choice is visible and comparative rather than the first idea that arrived.
4. Under a chosen opportunity, generate multiple competing solutions rather than committing to the first one anyone proposes.
5. For the solution the team leans toward, list the assumptions that must be true for it to work, and design the cheapest test for the assumption that's both most load-bearing and least evidenced.
6. Keep the tree alive as a living artifact — update it every week as interviews surface new opportunities or kill old assumptions, rather than building it once for a kickoff deck.

## Example

A team whose outcome is "reduce time-to-first-value for new signups" might interview recently onboarded users and surface three distinct opportunities — confusion about where to start, too many required setup steps, and unclear value before the first real use — each of which could spawn several different solutions; without the tree, a team tends to jump straight to "simplify the setup wizard" and never notices the other two branches existed.

## Common mistakes

- **Starting the tree from a solution instead of an outcome**: if the root is already "redesign onboarding," the tree just organizes justifications for a decision already made rather than genuinely exploring the opportunity space.
- **Mapping only one opportunity before jumping to solutions**: the tree's actual value is comparative — seeing several opportunities side by side is what reveals you picked the easiest one to talk about, not the highest-leverage one.
- **Treating it as a one-time diagram for a deck**: Torres's framing is explicitly continuous — a tree built once for a kickoff and never revisited stops reflecting what the team has actually learned.
- **Skipping assumption tests and going straight to building**: the leaves of the tree exist specifically to catch the load-bearing, least-evidenced beliefs before they get expensive to be wrong about.

## Limitations

- **Requires a genuinely continuous interview cadence to stay accurate**: without a weekly rhythm of real customer contact, the opportunity space calcifies and the tree becomes a static snapshot pretending to be current.
- **Doesn't itself prioritize across trees**: if a team has outcomes for three different initiatives, the framework doesn't tell you which outcome to invest in this quarter — that's a separate strategic call.
- **Can become a compliance artifact rather than a thinking tool**: teams asked to "have an OST" without buying into continuous discovery sometimes produce a tree-shaped document once and never touch it again, missing the entire point.

## Tips

Make the tree visible to the whole team, not just the researcher or PM who built it — its real power is letting an engineer or designer see, at a glance, which opportunities and solutions were considered and rejected, and why, so debates don't restart from zero every time someone proposes an old idea.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Clustering raw interview notes into candidate opportunity statements as a starting draft for the team to validate against the actual transcripts.
- Generating a wider set of candidate solutions under a chosen opportunity branch, to counteract the human tendency to stop at the first workable idea.

**Where it fails**
- Populating the opportunity space from synthetic or imagined customer input instead of real interviews. The entire mechanism depends on opportunities being sourced from actual conversations with actual customers — a model asked to "generate likely opportunities" for a persona will produce plausible-sounding branches that were never actually said by anyone, which defeats the method's purpose of surfacing what a team doesn't yet know.
