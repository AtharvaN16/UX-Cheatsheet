## What is it

The probability-weighted average of every possible outcome of a decision. A bet with a 30% chance of gaining $300 and a 70% chance of losing $50 has an expected value of $55 — positive, even though losing is the more likely single outcome.

## Why it matters

Expected value is the tool for making decisions by their probability-weighted outcome rather than by their most likely single outcome — and the two can point in opposite directions. A decision with positive expected value should generally be taken even when any individual instance of it produces a bad outcome; a decision with negative expected value is a bad bet even if it happens to pay off once. Annie Duke's framing — thinking in bets rather than certainties — is the practical translation of this into everyday judgment.

## Key ideas

- **Weight outcomes by probability, not vividness**: a rare catastrophic outcome and a common mild one both belong in the calculation, weighted by how likely each actually is — not by how memorable or scary they feel.
- **Positive EV can still lose; negative EV can still win**: any single instance of a bet is one draw from a distribution. Evaluating a past decision by whether that one draw went well is the resulting error — judging decision quality by outcome quality.
- **Variance matters alongside expected value**: two bets with the same expected value can have very different spreads of possible outcomes; a decision-maker also needs to ask whether they can survive the worst case, not just whether the average case is favorable.

## Example

A designer is deciding whether to ship a risky redesign before a big launch event. Estimated: 40% chance it lifts conversion by an amount worth $200K, 60% chance it causes a regression costing $60K in lost conversion during the launch window. EV = (0.4 × $200K) − (0.6 × $60K) = $80K − $36K = $44K positive — a case for shipping, even though the more likely single outcome (60%) is the loss.

## Application

- Use it explicitly when comparing two product bets with different upside/downside profiles, rather than defaulting to whichever has the more likely single outcome.
- Push a stakeholder conversation from "will this work?" to "what's the probability-weighted value across how it could go?" — this reframes disagreements about a single guess into disagreements about specific probabilities and payoffs, which are easier to debate productively.
- Pair it with a gut check on variance: a positive-EV bet that could bankrupt the team if it goes wrong may not be worth taking regardless of the average.

## Limitations

- **Garbage-in, garbage-out**: the calculation is only as good as the probability and value estimates feeding it, and those are often the most contested and least certain part of the whole exercise.
- **Doesn't capture risk tolerance**: a positive expected value doesn't mean a decision-maker should always take the bet — an asymmetric downside that's unrecoverable can outweigh a favorable average.
- **Assumes probabilities are knowable at all**: many real product and career decisions involve genuine uncertainty (Frank Knight's distinction) rather than known-probability risk, and forcing a false precision onto unknown probabilities produces misplaced confidence.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Negative outcome means it was a bad decision"**: this is resulting — the outcome of one instance of a positive-EV bet tells you little about whether the decision itself was sound.
- **"EV requires exact numbers"**: rough probability ranges are usually enough to compare two options meaningfully; the value of the exercise is forcing explicit estimates, not achieving false precision.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Structuring the calculation itself — laying out outcomes, probabilities, and values in a clear table and doing the arithmetic correctly and quickly.
- Stress-testing whether your probability estimates are internally consistent (e.g., do they sum to 100% across mutually exclusive outcomes).

**Where it fails**
- A model has no privileged access to the actual probabilities of your specific business or design outcome — a fluent, precise-looking EV calculation built on invented or unverified probability estimates gives false confidence in a number that's only as good as guesses dressed up as data.
