## What is it

A five-stage model of the customer lifecycle — Acquisition, Activation, Retention, Referral, Revenue (the acronym reads "AARRR," hence "pirate metrics") — giving startups a shared vocabulary for which numbers actually matter, stage by stage, instead of tracking everything and understanding nothing.

## When to use

- You're diagnosing where a product is losing people and need to isolate which stage of the lifecycle is actually broken, rather than treating "growth" as one undifferentiated problem
- You're setting up analytics for a new product and need a starting structure rather than instrumenting everything indiscriminately
- Different teams (marketing, onboarding, retention, monetization) need a shared frame that shows how their individual metrics connect into one lifecycle
- You want a fast, standard vocabulary for a metrics review without inventing a bespoke framework from scratch

## Structure

- **Acquisition**: how users arrive — channel, source, and cost per channel (SEO, paid, referral, PR).
- **Activation**: whether a new user has a "happy" first experience — usually defined as reaching a specific in-product moment, not just signing up.
- **Retention**: whether users come back — measured over a time window appropriate to the product's natural usage cadence.
- **Referral**: whether users like the product enough to bring others — organic sharing, invites, word-of-mouth.
- **Revenue**: whether any of the above converts to monetization — purchase, subscription, upgrade.

## How to use

1. Map your product's actual user journey onto the five stages before picking metrics — a B2B tool's "activation" moment looks nothing like a consumer app's, and forcing a generic definition produces meaningless numbers.
2. Pick one or two metrics per stage, not a dashboard's worth — McClure's own point was to focus attention, not create a wall of charts.
3. Instrument conversion rate *between* stages, not just volume within a stage — the drop-off from Activation to Retention is usually more diagnostic than either number alone.
4. Diagnose stage by stage: a product with strong Acquisition and weak Activation has a different problem (and a different owner) than one with strong Activation and weak Retention.
5. Treat Referral and Revenue as downstream of the first three — pushing referral incentives on a product with weak Retention just accelerates churn of the referred users too.

## Example

A product with healthy signups (Acquisition) but a steep drop before users complete a key first action (Activation) has a design problem in onboarding, not a marketing problem — pirate metrics forces that distinction instead of lumping both under a vague "growth is slow."

## Common mistakes

- **Treating it as a strict funnel instead of a lifecycle**: real users loop back through the stages (a retained user refers a friend, who needs their own activation) — modeling it as one-directional hides where loops could be reinforcing growth.
- **Copying generic stage definitions instead of mapping your own journey**: "Activation = signup" for a product whose real value only shows up three steps later produces a number that looks fine while users are actually failing.
- **Overloading each stage with too many metrics**: the framework's value is focus; five stages with ten metrics each recreates the "track everything, understand nothing" problem it was built to solve.
- **Ignoring Referral and Revenue until later**: teams that only track Acquisition and Activation build a product that gets people in the door without ever learning whether it's actually monetizable or worth talking about.

## Limitations

- **A linear model applied to what's often a compounding system**: growth-loops thinking (Balfour, Winters, Kwok, Chen) argues that funnels leak and need constant top-of-funnel spend, while the strongest growth today comes from loops where output reinvests into input — pirate metrics doesn't model that reinvestment on its own.
- **Says nothing about UX quality within a stage**: two products can have identical Activation rates for completely different reasons (one is genuinely delightful, one just has a low bar); it's a lifecycle-stage model, not a UX-quality model.
- **Stage boundaries get fuzzy in complex, multi-sided products**: marketplaces and platforms with two or more distinct user types (supply and demand sides) need the framework applied separately per side, which the original five-word acronym doesn't hint at.

## Tips

Use it as a diagnostic map to locate *where* to look, then bring in a stage-appropriate method for *why* — heuristic-evaluation or usability-testing for a broken Activation stage, contextual-inquiry for a weak Retention stage — rather than expecting the five letters alone to explain the cause.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Summarizing funnel-stage analytics into a plain-language diagnosis of where the steepest drop-off sits, as a starting hypothesis for a team to investigate further.
- Drafting stage-specific metric definitions tailored to a specific product's actual user journey, for a human to sanity-check against real behavior.

**Where it fails**
- Assuming the five generic stage labels map cleanly onto any product without someone first defining what each stage concretely means for that specific journey. A model asked to "set up AARRR tracking" will confidently propose generic events (signup = Acquisition, first login = Activation) that can be badly wrong for the actual value moment in your product.
