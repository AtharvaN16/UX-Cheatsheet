## What is it

The value of the best alternative given up by making a particular choice — the true cost of any choice isn't just what you spend, but the next-best thing you could have done with the same resources instead.

## Why it matters

Every decision has an opportunity cost, and it's almost always the most important cost that never appears on a budget line, a roadmap, or a time-tracking sheet. Asking "is this worth doing?" is the wrong question because almost anything is worth doing in isolation; the right question is "is this the best use of this time, money, or attention, given everything else currently available?"

## Key ideas

- **Cost isn't just what you spend, it's what you give up**: a "free" internal project still costs whatever the team would otherwise have built.
- **It's relative to the actual next-best alternative, not to doing nothing**: comparing a decision only against inaction understates its real cost when a better alternative was genuinely available.
- **It applies to attention as much as money**: a designer's time in a low-value meeting has an opportunity cost measured in whatever design work didn't happen instead — this is often invisible precisely because it never shows up as a line-item loss.

## Example

A design team spends a sprint building a highly-requested but low-usage settings page instead of fixing a checkout flow with a known 3% drop-off. The settings page "worked" — it shipped, it functions — but its real cost was the checkout fix that didn't happen, which is the actual opportunity cost of the decision, independent of whether the settings page itself succeeded.

## Application

- Reframe roadmap debates from "should we build this?" to "is this the best use of this team's time right now, given what else is on the list?" — the second question surfaces trade-offs the first one hides.
- When a stakeholder pushes for a "quick" feature, ask what it displaces, not just what it costs to build — the displaced work is often the larger cost.
- Use it explicitly when defending a "no" — naming the specific alternative use of the resources makes a rejection concrete rather than seeming arbitrary.

## Limitations

- **Requires a genuinely comparable alternative to name**: opportunity cost is meaningless as a critique if there's no realistic next-best use of the same resources — "we could have done anything" isn't a specific alternative.
- **Hard to quantify precisely**: unlike a cash cost, the value of a foregone alternative is usually an estimate, not a measured number, which makes it easy to inflate or dismiss depending on the argument someone wants to win.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Opportunity cost only applies to money"**: it applies to any scarce resource — attention, calendar time, design capacity, political capital — and is often larger for these than for direct cash cost.
- **"If something has value, it's worth doing"**: almost everything has some value in isolation; the question that matters is whether it has more value than the next-best alternative use of the same resource.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Generating a list of plausible alternative uses of a given resource (time, budget, team capacity) as a starting point for a real trade-off conversation.

**Where it fails**
- A model can name generic-sounding alternatives fluently, but it doesn't know your organization's actual backlog, capacity, or strategic priorities — the real opportunity cost is specific to context a model doesn't have, and a plausible-sounding "what you gave up" answer can be entirely wrong for your situation.
