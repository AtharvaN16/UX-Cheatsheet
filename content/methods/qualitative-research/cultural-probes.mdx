## What is it

A package of deliberately ambiguous artefacts — postcards carrying oblique questions, maps to annotate, a repackaged camera — left with participants for weeks and posted back piecemeal. Gaver, Dunne and Pacenti built the original set in 1999 for The Presence Project; their heading for it was "Inspiration, not Information".

## Purpose

Probes get designers past what a group already knows how to say about itself: a questionnaire returns answers shaped by its own questions, while probes pose tasks strange enough that the answer must be invented. What comes back is fragmentary and unrepresentative by construction — the point is to feed imagination, not settle a question.

## When to use

- The brief is genuinely open: you are deciding what to design, not which variant to ship
- The interesting material is attitudinal (pleasures, fears, aspirations) rather than procedural
- You can feel the pull toward a stereotype; the 1999 paper set out to reject older people as "needy" or "nice"

## How to do it

- **Design the artefacts for these people and this project.** Generic materials seem insincere, the authors write, "like official forms with a veneer of marketing".
- **Use oblique wording and evocative images.** Peccioli's postcards asked for a piece of advice that had mattered, or what place art had in people's lives.
- **Hand the package over in person without itemising it** — Gaver's team named only the *types* of thing inside — then leave it alone, taking returns as they trickle in, blanks included.
- **Interpret; do not analyse.** Returns go to the designers, who answer with proposals. The 2004 paper argues that summarising them produces an "average" reflecting no individual and filters out the unusual items that inspire most.
- **Take the proposals back to participants.** Recognising themselves in deliberately strange ideas is the sign the probes worked.

## Example

The Peccioli package held pre-stamped postcards, maps, a repackaged camera asking for "something desirable" and "something boring". One map was labelled "if Peccioli were New York...", with stickers from the Statue of Liberty to people injecting drugs. Oslo returned almost everything, Peccioli less than half — rates the team read as characterisation, not compliance failure. Three sites, three proposals, none from a coded finding.

## Common mistakes

- **Rationalising the probes.** Gaver and colleagues wrote a second paper about it: teams aim the tasks at specific questions, then summarise and analyse them into requirements. That makes the method a slow, tiny survey, and if the designers only see the summary the probes achieved nothing a questionnaire could not.
- **Buying a probe kit.** Reused artefacts carry no sign anyone made them for you, and get answered like junk mail.
- **Debriefing participants on what they meant.** Gaver's team deliberately didn't ask; resolving every ambiguity discards what was cultivated.

## Limitations

- **Returns are not evidence and cannot be converted into it.** They reflect the artefact as much as the participant, and the 2004 paper calls them impossible to analyse or interpret clearly.
- **Nothing generalises.** Gaver's later domestic study reached twenty households: nothing about a population, and scaling up will not change that.
- **Hard to defend in a commercial process.** No success metric, no sampling logic, no finding for a slide, and "how many people said that?" is unanswerable.
- **Quality rests on craft you may not have.** Materials must be considered but not too finished; miss that line and they read as childish or corporate.

## Tips

Write down what you hope the probes will unsettle before you send them, and read it when the returns arrive: probes work by defeating your framing, and that note is how you notice.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Generating a wide spread of oblique task prompts to choose from by hand; judgement sits in the selection
- Cataloguing and transcribing returns so the team can browse the raw material together, unreduced

**Where it fails**
- Interpreting the returns. Asked what a probe response means, a model gives a confident, tidy reading — precisely the rationalising Gaver warned against. It resolves ambiguity by default, and the ambiguity is the material.
