## What is it

A methodology for *generating* a theory from data rather than testing one or summarising what people said: you collect a little, analyse immediately, and let what emerged decide who you talk to next. The output explains a process, not a themed list.

## Why it matters

It is the most misused term in UX research. "We did grounded theory" nearly always means "we read our notes and found themes". That costs twice: it claims a rigour the work did not have, and it hides what grounded theory really offers — recruitment controlled by the analysis rather than the brief.

## Key ideas

- **Theoretical sampling** — who you recruit next fills gaps in the emerging categories, not fixed in advance. Applied work quietly drops this, and dropping it turns grounded theory into thematic analysis with a better-sounding name.
- **Constant comparison** — every new incident compared against earlier ones and against the codes, continuously, not at the end.
- **Coding upward** — incidents to codes, codes to categories, categories to one *core category* accounting for most of the variation.
- **Memo-writing** — analytic notes written while coding. Memos, not transcripts, are what the theory is written from.
- **Theoretical saturation** — you stop when more data stops altering the categories; a property of the categories, never a participant count.
- **Three schools, not one method** — Glaser held to emergence and warned against forcing data into a template; Strauss and Corbin added procedural scaffolding (axial coding, the coding paradigm); Charmaz treats theory as co-constructed rather than found in the data. The disagreements are substantive — name your school.

## Example

A team on a freight-brokerage tool runs four dispatcher interviews and gets an obvious theme: distrust of auto-assignment. Under theoretical sampling that theme picks the next participants — a dispatcher disciplined after a bad auto-assignment, a night-shift dispatcher with nobody to escalate to. Comparison yields the core category: dispatchers absorb blame for decisions the system will not explain. Thematic analysis would have stopped at "distrust of automation".

## Application

- Scope it as a programme, not a study: months, recruitment open, one school not three.
- Keep memos and coding decisions auditable; they are the only evidence the theory came from data.
- The honest partial adoption in product work is to borrow constant comparison and memo-writing, then call the result thematic analysis.

## Limitations

- **Saturation is claimed, never proven** — it holds only for categories you happened to build, and one nobody built cannot saturate.
- **It is incompatible with fixed scope.** Panels, procurement and sprint planning want a participant count up front; theoretical sampling refuses to give one.
- **The core category is a judgement** — two competent analysts on the same corpus can centre different processes and both defend it.
- **The unprimed stance is a fiction in applied work.** Glaserian GT asks you to delay the literature so categories are not forced; you already know your domain, so the condition cannot be met.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Grounded theory means coding interviews."** Coding is one component; without theoretical sampling and iteration to saturation, there is no grounded theory.
- **"Grounded just means bottom-up, so any inductive analysis counts."** Grounded means grounding a *theory* in data, not the direction of coding — propositions that explain a process and generate predictions you could be wrong about.

## Tips

Before reaching for the term, ask one question: did an early finding change who you recruited next? If not, you did thematic analysis. Saying so costs nothing; overclaiming costs the room's trust the moment someone asks how the sample was chosen.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Constant comparison at volume: surfacing later incidents that resemble or contradict a category you have memoed.
- Stress-testing a draft core category by asking for the cases it fails to explain.

**Where it fails**
- Producing the theory. Given a fixed set of transcripts a model returns a tidy core category and a confident saturation claim, because both are recognisable literary forms — neither earnable without fieldwork that has not happened.
