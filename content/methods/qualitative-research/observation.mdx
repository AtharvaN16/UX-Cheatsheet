## What is it

Watching behaviour happen and recording what you see, without asking the person to narrate or explain it. It is the technique underneath the family of field methods: fly-on-the-wall, shadowing, contextual inquiry and ethnography are each observation with a particular observer role, duration and licence to interrupt. Those have their own cards; this one is the common core.

## Purpose

Observation gets the parts of behaviour people cannot report: steps automated years ago and no longer noticed, the workaround they stopped calling a workaround, what interrupts them. Self-report is a reconstruction; observation is a record.

## When to use

- The behaviour is habitual, physical or fast enough that people cannot narrate it accurately
- You need real frequencies, timings or error counts from the wild rather than a lab task
- You have a suspected problem but not a describable one, and need to see the failure to write the research question

## How to do it

- Decide your observer role before you arrive. The spectrum above is that decision: involvement buys access to meaning and costs you an unaltered scene.
- Write a protocol naming what counts as an event worth recording — without one, notes from five sites are five incommensurable stories, and two observers cannot be compared.
- Pick a sampling scheme: *continuous* for windows under an hour, *time sampling* for a snapshot every few minutes, *event sampling* for each occurrence of a defined trigger — usually the right default.
- Keep description and inference separate — two columns, or two colours. Inference mixed into the record cannot be removed later.

## Example

A tablet app for hotel housekeeping tested clean and fast in the lab. Two mornings of event-sampled observation on a real floor — trigger: every touch of the tablet — found it face-down on the cart all shift: both hands occupied, the screen locked after thirty seconds, statuses entered per corridor from memory. Nobody had reported it; from the inside that was the job, not an app problem. The fix was a longer lock timeout and a cart mount, neither of them a screen.

## Common mistakes

- **Writing interpretations as observations** — "she was frustrated" is a conclusion; "she re-scanned the same barcode four times, then said 'come on'" is data you can still argue with.
- **Drifting into an interview** — ask questions if you decide to, but decide; by accident you no longer know which behaviour was spontaneous.
- **Observing only where access was easy** — the compliant team volunteers; the site running the interesting workaround declines.
- **One session treated as the setting** — a Tuesday morning is not the week, but one window becomes "how they work" when no second window contradicts it.

## Limitations

- **Behaviour without reasons.** Observation is silent on motivation, tradeoffs and the option someone rejected; getting those means switching methods.
- **Your presence changes the scene.** Being watched shifts behaviour toward the version people believe is correct. Lower-involvement roles reduce this; nothing removes it.
- **Rare and private behaviour is out of reach.** Anything happening monthly, at 2am, or alone cannot be waited out at any realistic budget.
- **The subjectivity moved into the coding.** A protocol makes notes comparable, but you chose the categories before you knew what you would see, and whatever fits none gets quietly dropped.

## Tips

Write up the same day. Field notes are shorthand only you can decode, and the context that made them legible is gone by the weekend.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Turning timestamped notes from several sessions into one comparable event log, flagging lines that slid from description into inference
- Drafting a candidate code list, then checking it for categories that overlap or cannot be told apart live

**Where it fails**
- Coding behaviour from video: a model labels a pause "hesitation" and a re-scan "confusion", unable to tell either from a colleague walking past — coded observational data without the observer who knew what else was in the room.
