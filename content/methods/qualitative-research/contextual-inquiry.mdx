## What is it

You go to where someone actually does the work — their desk, their warehouse floor, their kitchen — and watch them do it while asking questions in the moment, rather than asking them to recall it afterward in an interview room.

## Purpose

People are bad at accurately describing their own workflows from memory: they forget steps, rationalize workarounds, and describe the process they intend to follow rather than the one they actually use. Watching in context surfaces the gap between stated and actual behavior.

## When to use

- The task is complex, expert-level, or embedded in a physical or organizational environment you can't easily reconstruct in an interview room
- You suspect there's a meaningful gap between what people say they do and what they actually do
- You're designing for a workflow, not a single screen — contextual inquiry shows you the work before and after your product, not just inside it

## How to do it

1. Use the master-apprentice model: position yourself as an apprentice being taught the work, not an examiner testing the person. This framing gets people to narrate things they'd otherwise consider too obvious to mention.
2. Observe and interview simultaneously — ask about what's happening as it happens, not in a debrief afterward.
3. Interpret collaboratively: check your understanding of what you just saw with the person in the moment, rather than assuming and finding out you were wrong later.
4. Visit 4-6 people doing the work, prioritizing variety (different roles, tenure, or context) over raw count.
5. Take detailed notes on the physical environment, tools, interruptions, and workarounds — these are usually the most valuable and least self-reported detail.
6. Synthesize across visits into a shared model of the work (sequence, artifacts, culture) rather than a report per individual.

## Common mistakes

- **Interviewing about the work instead of watching it** — if you're not physically present while the work happens, you're doing a user interview with extra steps, not a contextual inquiry.
- **Correcting or teaching during the visit** — the master-apprentice framing breaks the moment you start explaining a "better way"; you're there to learn the current reality, not to fix it live.
- **Only visiting the smoothest performers** — the person who's found a clever workaround for a broken process is often more valuable to observe than the person following the process exactly as designed.
- **Skipping the physical environment** — noting only what's on screen misses the sticky notes, the second monitor, the phone call that interrupts every third task — often where the real workflow lives.

## Tips

Budget more calendar time than headcount suggests — a single good site visit can run 1.5-2 hours, and travel/scheduling logistics dominate the timeline more than the interviews themselves.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Structuring raw field notes (artifacts, sequence, interruptions) into a shared work model across multiple visits
- Drafting the interpretation-session questions you'll use to check your read of a visit with a colleague who wasn't there

**Where it fails**
- Inferring a workflow from a screen recording or log data alone. Contextual inquiry's value is specifically the physical and social context around the screen — a model working only from digital traces will confidently reconstruct a workflow that omits exactly the environmental detail the method exists to capture.
