## What is it

A one-on-one, guided conversation with someone in your target audience, asking about their experiences, behaviors, and needs — not about your product's usability, which is what usability testing is for.

## Purpose

Surfaces context, motivation, and language you can't get from analytics or a survey: why someone does what they do, what they were trying to accomplish, what they compared you against, and what words they naturally use to describe the problem.

## When to use

- Early in a project, to understand a problem space before you've designed anything
- When you need to know *why* a behavior happens, not just that it happens (analytics tells you the what)
- When you need the vocabulary your users actually use, for later use in copy, labels, or a survey

## How to do it

1. Write a discussion guide: 8-12 open-ended questions organized loosely by topic, not a rigid script.
2. Recruit 5-8 participants per distinct user segment — saturation (new interviews stop surfacing new themes) usually arrives in that range for a well-scoped question.
3. Ask about past, specific behavior ("tell me about the last time you...") rather than hypotheticals ("would you ever...") — people are unreliable narrators of their own future behavior but reasonably reliable about a specific recent instance.
4. Use the funnel technique: start broad and open, narrow toward specifics only after the participant has described things in their own words.
5. Record (with consent) and take notes on a second pass rather than trying to capture everything live — divided attention makes for worse follow-up questions.
6. Synthesize across interviews by pulling recurring themes, not by treating any single participant's story as representative.

## Common mistakes

- **Asking leading or hypothetical questions** — "wouldn't it be great if..." invites agreement, not insight. Ask about the past, not a hypothetical future.
- **Pitching your solution mid-interview** — turns a research conversation into a sales pitch and contaminates every answer that follows.
- **Treating one compelling quote as validation** — a vivid story from one participant is a hypothesis, not a finding, until it recurs.
- **Under-scoping the guide** — a guide with 25 questions produces shallow answers to all of them; fewer, better questions produce depth.

## Tips

The best interviewers say less than the participant expects. Silence after a participant finishes speaking, held for even 3-4 seconds, often produces the most useful follow-on detail — most people fill silence rather than let it sit.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Drafting and stress-testing a discussion guide for leading questions before you run it
- Transcribing and timestamping recordings, so later synthesis can search by phrase rather than re-listening

**Where it fails**
- Synthetic participants — asking a model to simulate "what a user would say" in an interview. It returns the statistical median of its training data dressed as a specific person's voice, which is the opposite of what an interview is for: finding the specific, surprising thing a real person says that you didn't expect.
