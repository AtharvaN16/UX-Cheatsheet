## What is it

Six to eight people from a target audience in a moderated discussion against a topic guide. The unit of analysis is the group, not the person: the data is what gets said *and* how the room reacts. Erika Hall's advice in *Just Enough Research*: never, ever hold one.

## Purpose

The one thing no one-on-one gives you: opinions formed and defended in front of peers — which words survive challenge, which objections get conceded, where consensus is real and where social. Krueger and Casey insist the output is the *range* of views, never a count.

## When to use

- You need the arguments a community makes to each other, and the vocabulary that survives them
- The decision being designed for is itself collective — a team or a committee
- You don't yet know which questions deserve a one-on-one hour

Not for usability, preference measurement, feature validation, or anything people want to look competent about.

## How to do it

- Segment hard: homogeneous within a group, varied across them.
- Ten questions for 90 minutes; with seven people that is a minute each per question — your ceiling on depth.
- Collect written answers *before* discussion, so you know who moved.
- Say disagreement is the point, then moderate for it: round by seat, the silent invited by name, a third consecutive turn cut off.
- Never demo the product — Nielsen's point is that the session becomes a demo, and watching one tells you nothing about use.
- Run three groups per segment and analyse *across* them; a theme from one group is a property of that room.

## Example

Three groups of seven care coordinators, for a team building home-care rota software. Coordinators say "a run", never "a route", and every group split loudly over whether last-minute reassignment is scheduling or staffing — a fault line one-on-ones return as two contradictory accounts. The same groups were unanimously keen on a mobile approval flow; usability testing later showed they would not leave the desktop rota.

## Common mistakes

- **Validating a design in a group** — a room, a demo and a hopeful designer produce consensus that predicts nothing.
- **Reading agreement as signal** — agreeing is cheaper than dissenting, so convergence is partly an artefact of speaking order.
- **Mixing hierarchy in one group** — nobody names their own workaround in front of whoever wrote the policy.
- **Counting hands** — "five of eight preferred B", from people who heard each other choose, is not preference data but gets quoted as such.

## Limitations

- **Conformity cannot be undone in analysis.** Once views converge, no one's prior position is recoverable: a participant's view and the room's effect on it are inseparable. Written answers first mitigates that; it does not fix it.
- **Airtime goes by personality.** The dominant participant is over-represented, the quiet one absent, and absence leaves no trace to correct for.
- **The stated/actual gap is worse than in interviews.** Participants manage an impression for peers too, so money, hygiene, competence and skipped steps come back sanitised — and you cannot watch eight people use something at once to check it.
- **Your n is groups, not people.** Three groups of eight is n=3 for anything the group influenced, so interview sample-size intuition overstates it eightfold.
- **Moderation decides the result**, and is invisible afterwards: a badly moderated transcript reads like a good one.

## Tips

If the appeal is recruiting efficiency, run five one-on-ones instead — similar hours, attributable data, no conformity effect.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Diarising the recording and reporting airtime per speaker — dominance becomes something you can show the stakeholder citing the consensus
- Screening a draft guide for questions that can only produce a show of hands

**Where it fails**
- Synthetic focus groups: a model role-playing eight participants manufactures the convergence without the people, precisely the artefact that makes real groups untrustworthy. Simulated participants have no interests to defend, so they never argue.
