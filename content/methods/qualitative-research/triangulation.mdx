## What is it

Attacking one question with sources that fail in different ways, then reading the pattern of agreement and disagreement between them. Denzin named four types — *data*, *investigator*, *theory* and *methodological*.

## Why it matters

Every method has a characteristic failure: analytics cannot say why, interviews rely on self-report, usability tests watch a task nobody chose. Triangulation trades one blind spot against another, so its value sits in the *independence* of the errors, not the number of sources.

## Key ideas

- **Data triangulation** — same method, other times, settings and people; catches artefacts of when you happened to look.
- **Investigator triangulation** — two researchers analyse the same material independently, so one analyst's expectations do not become the finding.
- **Theory triangulation** — one dataset read through rival explanatory frames; some behaviours only one frame explains.
- **Methodological triangulation** — different methods on one question; Denzin's *between-method* is the useful case, *within-method* the weaker one.
- **Divergence is informative.** Convergence is the cheap outcome. Disagreement means one of three things: the question means something different inside each instrument, the phenomenon is conditional on something you were not varying, or one instrument is broken. All three are findings; none is an error to average away.
- **Independent failure modes are the selection rule.** Two studies drawn from your logged-in users share the same missing population.

## Example

A cancellation flow. Assume analytics show 40% of users who open Cancel never complete it, read internally as retention. A survey of that group finds almost all meant to cancel; five sessions show they stop because nothing confirms the charge will stop, then cancel by email next day. Each measured a different thing, so the output is a corrected question, not an average: how many intended cancellations complete through *any* channel?

## Application

- Write the question down first and name what each source can and cannot see about it.
- Choose the second for a different failure mode, not for volume — if you cannot say how it fails differently, it is not one.
- Report divergences explicitly, with the reading you took and why — a reading you commit to before you can see which strand flatters the roadmap.

## Limitations

- **It cannot fix a badly framed question.** Four sources answering the wrong question agree beautifully and mislead in unison.
- **Shared bias reads exactly like corroboration.** A survey and an interview are both self-report; nothing in the results separates the two, so only the design tells you which you have.
- **There is no arithmetic for combining sources.** Integration is a judgement you defend in prose, not a weighted confidence figure.
- **Cost scales with sources, confidence does not.** The second source buys most of the gain; the fourth buys very little.

## Common misconceptions

- **"More sources, therefore more true."** Confidence comes from errors being uncorrelated; a correlated source adds only cost.
- **"Disagreement means the research failed."** It usually means the sources measured different things — the most useful thing you learned.
- **"Triangulation establishes validity."** Patton frames it as a credibility strategy for one claim, not a proof procedure.
- **"It is the same as mixed methods."** A mixed-methods design decides sequence, weighting and sampling before collection; triangulation is the interpretive move afterwards.
- **"You need three."** Nothing in Denzin requires three; the name is a surveying metaphor, not a count.

## Tips

Before opening the second source, write down which result would change your mind about the first. If none would, you are collecting support.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Tabulating what each source actually measured, surfacing shared-bias overlaps before you read agreement as corroboration.
- Enumerating rival explanations for a divergence, including the dull possibility that an instrument is broken.

**Where it fails**
- Reconciling the strands. Given a survey, an analytics extract and session notes, a model produces a smooth account in which everything agrees, because coherence is what it optimises for — smoothing exactly the divergence that carried the information.
