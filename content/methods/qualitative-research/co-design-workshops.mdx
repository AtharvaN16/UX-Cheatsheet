## What is it

A facilitated session where the people who do the work *make* things alongside designers rather than discuss them: arranging component cards, filling blanks, acting a scenario out. Sanders, Brandt and Binder sort these into telling, making and enacting; the making separates this from a group interview with craft supplies.

## Purpose

Asked "what would help?", people answer from inside the current system. Given components and a blank board they lay the work out as they experience it, and the arrangement carries reasoning the spoken answer didn't.

## When to use

- The question is still open and the team can still change direction
- The work is specialist enough that your team cannot generate credible options alone
- Two groups describe the same process incompatibly — clinicians and coders — and a decision keeps stalling

## How to do it

- Pick one question the session can answer; two produce two shallow halves.
- Read the decision owner's note on what the outputs feed and what is fixed aloud in the room.
- Recruit people who do the work: mix role and tenure, include the sceptic.
- Build materials to the question: a card per existing thing, blanks for what doesn't, a board carrying the structure you want tested. Send a sensitizing task a week ahead.
- Run making, not talking: groups of two or three produce an artifact, then present what they made and why. That presentation is the data — capture it with the board.
- Tell participants afterwards what changed and what didn't.

## Example

A hospital pharmacy reworking the discharge-medication checklist, on a timeline board marked with ward rounds and the pharmacy cut-off. All three pharmacist-and-nurse pairs moved medicines reconciliation earlier than the official process puts it — the cut-off makes the official order impossible on discharge-heavy afternoons. Nobody had mentioned it in interviews: too routine to say, visible only once the day was laid out.

## Common mistakes

- **Booking the session after the decisions are made.** Once platform and scope are fixed, a workshop can only ratify — and it feels identical from inside the room, producing outputs the team cannot act on.
- **No commitment to the output.** If the team is free to ignore what gets made, the session is participation theatre — Arnstein's tokenism rungs with better stationery.
- **Letting it become a group interview.** Ask a circle what they think and you get the loudest opinion plus a room nodding; if nobody's hands are busy, the method isn't running.
- **Shipping the sticky notes.** Notes moved into the backlog without their rationale lose the only part that was evidence.

## Limitations

- **It cannot make a project participatory.** A format cannot redistribute authority; whether the session mattered was settled beforehand, by whoever owns the decision.
- **Group dynamics suppress minority positions.** You get what the group will defend out loud: seat a manager with their reports and you get the manager's design. Working hours also select for whoever can leave their post.
- **The output is not shippable design.** Participants build from present constraints and carry none of the technical consequences, so a board treated as a spec ships their workarounds.
- **Facilitation shapes the result more than the method admits.** The same materials in two facilitators' hands produce different boards, and nothing separates your framing from their contribution.

## Tips

The ratio of pre-printed cards to blanks sets how much invention the room feels licensed to do: mostly pre-printed reads as validation, mostly blanks leaves people staring.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Drafting the kit from a process map: card sets, board structures, sensitizing task.
- Cross-reading rationale from several groups to find the explanation that recurred across tables, not the most vivid one.

**Where it fails**
- Generating the output. Ask a model what a co-design session with pharmacists would surface and it returns clean, plausible themes with no artifact and no participant behind them. That reads like good synthesis, so it gets presented as the room's conclusion, when the method's only evidence is specific people making specific things for stated reasons.
