## What is it

A commitment about who decides, not a session you book. People who will live with a system have a standing right to shape it, and adopting that changes a project's governance. It comes out of 1970s Scandinavian workplace democracy: Ehn and Kyng's UTOPIA project put typographers' union representatives on the team designing the tools that would reshape their trade.

## Why it matters

It makes two claims, and they come apart. The *political* claim: people affected by a change should hold power over it. The *epistemic* claim: tacit knowledge of the work — the reason the official process is abandoned on Fridays — lives in the practitioner and degrades when relayed through a researcher's notes.

Teams routinely accept the second and decline the first, and that gap is where participation theatre lives: materials, a warm session, no transfer of authority.

## Key ideas

- **Mutual learning.** Designers learn the work; participants learn what the technology can and cannot do. Hence the tradition's mock-ups and props rather than specs — nobody participates in wireframes and story points.
- **Participation is a ladder, not a switch.** Arnstein puts informing and consultation on the tokenism rungs, partnership, delegated power and citizen control on the rungs above. Your rung is set by what participants can *veto*, not by how collaborative the room feels.
- **The affected group is wider than the user.** Anyone whose work, pay, autonomy or safety changes has standing — including those the system makes redundant.

## Example

A logistics company replacing the paper run sheet drivers fill in at each drop. Consultative: shadow drivers, run a feedback session, ship — their point that the sheet doubles as evidence in disputed-delivery claims dies in the backlog because nobody at the scope cut knew what it was for. Participatory: two drivers elected by their depot hold a written veto over proof of delivery, and the same fact becomes a constraint nobody can drop. Authority, not technique.

## Application

- Before recruiting anyone, write the list of decisions participants can change and get whoever owns each to sign it. Empty list, no participation — call it research.
- Get participation in before the choices that foreclose everything else: platform, data model, business model, buyer.

## Limitations

- **It cannot manufacture authority you don't have.** A designer without sponsorship can run every technique in the canon and still deliver tokenism: the rung is set by the org chart, not the facilitation.
- **No tie-breaker for real conflicts of interest.** When management wants throughput and workers want predictable hours, participation surfaces the conflict and hands it back unresolved. Scandinavian practice had unions and labour law; a product team has neither.
- **Representation is unsolved.** Participants are volunteers, and co-deciding a system that may cut your own hours is a heavier ask than an interview: the people it would cut hardest decline most often.

## Common misconceptions

- **"We ran a co-design workshop, so the project is participatory."** The workshop is a format. If the team is free to ignore the outputs, or the session came after the platform was chosen, the output is decoration and the rung is tokenism.
- **"Users design the product."** Designers still do the design work, without sole authority over what it is for.
- **"A nicer name for user research."** Research extracts knowledge and leaves the decision where it was; this moves it. The techniques overlap almost entirely, hence the confusion.

## Tips

The fastest honesty test on a project claiming to be participatory: name one decision reversed because participants said so. If nobody can, it is consultation, whatever the deck calls it.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Reading a brief against the delivery plan to flag decisions already locked, so the list of changeable decisions is honest.
- Keeping participants' wording attached to a requirement as it travels into tickets, so the reason a constraint exists outlives whoever heard it.

**Where it fails**
- Simulated stakeholder panels. A model produces a plausible transcript of nurses discussing your design: the shape of consent without anyone having consented. The claim is about power, and no model transfers any.
