## What is it

You watch a setting without taking part in it and without interacting with anyone: no questions, no prompts, no clarifying. The unit of study is a place and what happens in it — a clinic waiting room, a ticket hall at rush hour — not a person.

## Purpose

Behaviour in a shared setting is produced by the setting: where people queue, what they ask a stranger rather than the machine. Removing the researcher removes the main thing that distorts it: whatever you see, the place produced it, not your question. NN/g places this at the observational end of the field-study spectrum.

## When to use

- You want base rates before you have a hypothesis worth interviewing: how many approach the kiosk versus the staffed desk
- The work cannot be interrupted: a control room, a trading desk, a kitchen mid-service
- A present researcher would erase the behaviour you are after: skipping a check, sharing a login, jumping a queue

## How to do it

- Fix the frame before you go: boundary, time windows, and the behaviours you will count, or you come back with atmosphere rather than data. Sample time as deliberately as place — 08:10 and 14:40 on the same concourse are two different studies.
- Pick a vantage point where watching is unremarkable and stay put. Record structurally: floor-plan marks for where behaviour happens, tallies for how often.
- Write only what is observable: "glanced at the sign, then joined the desk queue", not "was confused by the sign". Keep inferences in a separate column.
- Run the first window with two observers and compare tallies; disagreement means the definitions are too loose.

## Example

A transit operator's new ticket machines tested cleanly in the lab and still left a queue at the staffed window. Two researchers watched one concourse in 45-minute windows across a week, marking approach paths on a floor plan. Arrivals from the main entrance met the machines on a diagonal that hid the card reader behind a pillar; many looked, paused and carried on to the window. The fix was which way the machines faced, not what was on screen.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating "public" as consent.** A hospital waiting room, a school corridor and a staff canteen are not public in the sense that matters. Get the site owner's permission in writing, post a visible notice saying who you are and how to opt out, and capture no identifying detail — no photographs, no names. If the study only works because people don't know it is happening, it needs an ethics review, not a better vantage point.
- **Sliding from a place to a person** — following one shopper around a store is surveillance. When someone leaves the frame, you stop.
- **Filling in the why.** You cannot ask, so every motive in your notes is invention that later gets quoted as an interview finding.

## Limitations

- **No access to intent, ever.** One person abandons a kiosk because it is broken, another because they remembered they need a printed receipt; from across the room the two are identical, and no amount of further watching separates them.
- **Findings are bound to the setting.** They hold for that concourse, that layout, that hour; another site is a new study, not a replication.
- **Tallies invite over-reading.** A count from six windows describes those windows; as a percentage it reads like a measured rate.

## Tips

Log near-misses as carefully as completions: the hand that reaches for the kiosk and withdraws, the pause before choosing a queue. Hesitations mark what to probe in interviews or shadowing afterwards.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Tightening behaviour definitions into mutually exclusive categories, where observer disagreement is prevented
- Consolidating floor plans and tally sheets into a summary of where behaviour concentrated

**Where it fails**
- Explaining what you saw. Ask a model why people bypassed the kiosk and it returns a ranked list of plausible causes in the register of a findings summary — the one output this method cannot produce.
