## What is it

A research tradition, not a session type: extended time in a community or setting, describing it from the inside in its own categories and words — how life there hangs together, not findings about a task. The fieldwork is observation and unstructured conversation; the commitments make it ethnography: immersion over time, the emic frame, thick description, reflexivity about your effect on what you were shown.

## Purpose

Ethnography answers the question you do not yet know how to ask — a new market, a profession whose priorities you have only inferred — returning categories worth designing against, not a backlog.

Geertz's thick description explains why the time is necessary: a wink and a twitch are physically identical and socially opposite, so an account stopping at behaviour records the wrong thing faithfully.

## When to use

- Entering a domain your team has no lived knowledge of, where significant and incidental behaviour look alike
- The unit of interest is a group, role or household rather than an individual — a nursing shift, a family's finances
- The decision at stake is strategic; at sprint scale this is the wrong method

## How to do it

- Negotiate access through a person — vouched for, renegotiated as you go, revocable, not a panel booking.
- Be there often enough to become boring; the behaviour that appears once you stop counting as a visitor is the reason for the timeline.
- Write field notes the same day, keeping what happened separate from what you made of it.
- Work toward emic categories: record the words people use, especially those mapping onto no concept you brought.
- Keep a reflexive log: who introduced you, whose side you appear to be on. Hammersley and Atkinson treat this as data — your position determines what you were shown.

## Example

A team building home-care scheduling software had modelled the domain as assigning carers to visits. Six weeks across three agencies found the real work elsewhere: which carers a client would accept, and which pairs must never meet — kept in a private notebook, because writing it into a system felt indiscreet. Nobody volunteers what they believe they should not write down.

## Common mistakes

- **Relabelling a site visit** — a two-day field trip is observation; calling it ethnography promises a cultural account and delivers anecdote.
- **Staffing it with a rota** — rapport accrues to a person, so five researchers doing a day each produce five first visits.
- **Never writing the account** — the deliverable is a synthesised description; raw notes get read as a mood board and quoted selectively.
- **Ignoring your own access route** — if the manager walked you in, you saw the compliant version; treating it as the setting bakes your introduction into the findings.

## Limitations

- **It cannot be shortened without becoming another method.** The commitments are temporal; cut the timeline and you have field observation under an inflated name.
- **The account does not generalise.** It claims depth about one setting; a second site can contradict the first with both correct.
- **Findings resist the formats organisations decide in.** A cultural description reduces to neither a ticket nor a metric, which is why commissioned ethnography gets shelved.
- **The interpretation is the researcher's and not reproducible.** Thick description argues about meaning rather than measuring it; a colleague with different access returns a different account.

## Tips

The usual failure is unanalysed fieldwork. Analyse as you go, so later weeks chase what the early weeks raised, and budget write-up on the order of field time.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Indexing weeks of field notes, surfacing where a term you treated as one concept is used two ways
- Reading a draft account back as an outsider, surfacing etic framing you imported without noticing

**Where it fails**
- Writing the account. Asked to describe a profession's culture, a model returns its received public image — the stereotype immersion exists to displace — in fluent thick-description prose. It cannot be reflexive, having occupied no position.
