## What is it

The participant keeps up a running commentary while working — what they see, what they expect, what they are about to try — while the facilitator stays quiet. Ericsson and Simon set the rule that still defines it: ask for a report of thoughts as they occur, *not* for explanation or justification.

## Purpose

Supplies the reasoning behind an action at the moment it happens. It turns "they hesitated here" into "they hesitated because".

## When to use

- Inside a formative usability test, where diagnosing causes matters more than measuring anything
- When the sample is too small for statistics anyway, so reactivity costs you nothing
- When you need the words users reach for to name things on screen

## How to do it

- Instruct once, narrowly: keep saying whatever goes through your head, don't explain it, there are no wrong thoughts. A twenty-second demonstration beats describing it.
- When the participant goes quiet, nudge with something content-free — "keep talking".
- Log where the silences fall. They cluster on the hard moments, so silence is data.
- Read the verbalisation against the observed behaviour, never on its own; the disagreements between the two channels matter most.
- Don't report task time. Hertzum's meta-analysis of 42 concurrent-versus-retrospective comparisons found task time reliably longer under concurrent thinking aloud.
- Keep tasks short — van den Haak and colleagues found reactivity sharpest at high task complexity.

## Example

Suppose a payments team tests a scheduled-transfer flow with six participants thinking aloud. Watched silently, the repeated pause on the confirmation screen looks like reading. The narration says otherwise: most voice some version of "wait, has this already gone?" — the heading reads *Transfer scheduled* while the primary button still reads *Confirm*.

## Common mistakes

- **Asking "why?" during the task** — the most common violation of the original protocol. You get a theory instead of the behaviour's antecedent, and the participant becomes a commentator on their own session.
- **Filling silence with conversation** — every facilitator utterance steers attention toward whatever you just mentioned.
- **Treating the verbalisation as the finding** — people report intention, rarely mechanism. It is a lead, not a conclusion.
- **Running it on long, high-workload tasks** — twenty minutes of forced narration yields fatigue, thinning commentary, and a performance nothing like the real one.

## Limitations

- **It distorts what it measures.** Speaking competes for the same working memory as the task, so timings inflate and strategies simplify. Any number here describes someone narrating a task, not doing one, which also rules out eye-tracking and quantitative rounds.
- **Narrating can generate the insight it appears to observe.** Articulating a half-formed thought sometimes resolves it, so a participant may "notice" a label they would have missed in silence — invisible in the transcript.
- **Incomplete by construction.** Only what is in working memory and available to language gets said, and automated expert behaviour is exactly what does not verbalise. Weakest on tasks power users have internalised.
- **Unevenly demanding.** Narration is harder in a second language, at lower verbal fluency, and with some speech or cognitive differences; a sparse protocol there is about the participant, not the design.

## Tips

Treat the first task as a throwaway warm-up. Narration is a skill nobody practises, and the opening minutes are more self-conscious performance than thought.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Transcribing and timestamping sessions against screen-recording events, so you can jump to the moment behind a quote
- Flagging every facilitator utterance in a transcript, making protocol violations countable between sessions

**Where it fails**
- Generating a think-aloud transcript from a design or a synthetic persona. A model produces fluent narration for a screen no one has used. The real thing was evidence because the confusion was involuntary; a generated protocol is only the model's prior about interfaces — the intuition this method exists to check.
