## What is it

An interview with someone who holds specialist knowledge of the domain — a customs broker, a clinical coder — run to acquire that knowledge, not to study the expert as a user. An expert gives you a model of the domain; a user gives you their own experience. Bogner separates orienting in a new field, extracting its rules, and testing your own model.

## Purpose

In a specialised domain you cannot write a discussion guide, scenario or screener until you know what the work involves. Expert interviews buy that literacy in hours, and separate law from industry convention from one company's habit — a distinction nobody inside states, because everyone there knows it.

## When to use

- The domain has its own vocabulary, certifications or regulations
- You need to know what is legally or technically non-negotiable before scoping
- A workflow spans roles you cannot reach yet
- Stakeholders keep citing "how the industry works" and you cannot check it

## How to do it

- Map what you need from whom: rules and edge cases sit with practitioners, process shape with whoever coordinates it. One expert rarely holds both.
- Read first — an expert answers at the level your questions imply.
- Anchor every question in a real case — "the last declaration you had to argue about" — and ask for the artefact.
- Chase the exceptions. The canonical path is documented; exception handling is the part only a person knows, and where products break.
- Interview at least two experts likely to disagree; where they diverge, your design has a real decision. Keep a glossary in their phrasing and read it back.

## Example

A team building customs-declaration software interviewed two licensed brokers before writing a research plan. Liability for a wrong commodity code sits with the declarant, not the software, so the planned "auto-classify on upload" became a suggestion with a mandatory named sign-off. The two also disagreed on when to request a ruling — a call the product had treated as a lookup.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating the expert as a proxy user** — someone can explain how claims adjudication works and be wrong about how the twelve adjudicators downstairs spend their morning.
- **Letting the expert specify the interface** — they design for their own fluency, the wrong reference point for everyone else.
- **Interviewing only the most senior person** — seniority correlates with describing the official process, and with having stopped doing the work.
- **Not separating rule from habit** — "you can't do that" gets logged as a constraint when it was a preference, and a real constraint gets designed away.
- **One expert, treated as the domain** — you cannot tell an industry norm from one person's practice.

## Limitations

- **Experts describe the domain normatively.** You get the process as it should run, cleaned of shortcuts, because naming them implicates the expert. Watching the work is the only correction.
- **Expertise creates blind spots.** What an expert no longer experiences as a step is what will defeat your users, and the interview cannot surface it.
- **The expert has interests.** Someone whose professional value rests on a skill you propose to automate answers honestly inside a frame that protects it.
- **No sample validity.** Nothing generalises to users: this is domain knowledge, not evidence about behaviour, and filing it under "findings" gets it cited as though it were.

## Tips

Ask for "the weirdest one you've handled this year", then ask for the file. The story gives you the exception; the artefact gives you the fields, codes and states nobody would have listed.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Pre-reading public regulation, standards and trade documentation, so the expert's hour goes on judgement, not definitions.
- Screening your guide for questions already answerable from those documents.

**Where it fails**
- Standing in for the expert. A model is fluent on the canonical case and equally fluent on local convention and exceptions — where it reconstructs plausible detail rather than reporting practice. Nothing marks which is which, and those exceptions are why you wanted an expert.
