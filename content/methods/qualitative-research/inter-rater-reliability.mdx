## What is it

The degree to which two or more people independently applying the same codebook to the same data assign the same codes, reported as a chance-corrected coefficient rather than a raw percentage.

## Why it matters

A claim built on codes is only as stable as the coding: if a second analyst would code the transcripts differently, the finding is an artefact of who analysed it.

## Key ideas

### Percent agreement overstates reliability

Coders working at random still agree often. Cohen's illustration: two psychiatrists each labelling 10% of admissions schizophrenic, blindly and at random, jointly "agree" on 0.1 × 0.1 + 0.9 × 0.9 = 82% of cases — zero reliability. Every coefficient below subtracts that baseline.

### Which coefficient

- **Cohen's kappa** — two raters, nominal codes, one shared coding sheet.
- **Fleiss' kappa** — a fixed number of ratings per item by raters who need not be the same each time; the normal case once coding is spread across a team.
- **Krippendorff's alpha** — any number of coders, incomplete data, and ordinal or interval codes, where kappa assumes nominal categories and no gaps.

### The kappa paradox

Feinstein and Cicchetti showed high observed agreement can yield a low kappa when prevalence is skewed: if 95% of utterances are "not a complaint", chance agreement is near-total and a careful pair scores badly. Rare codes are where this bites.

## Example

A team codes 400 free-text cancellation reasons into six categories, two analysts double-coding 80. Raw agreement is 86%, but the price category dominates, so chance agreement is high and Cohen's kappa lands at 0.58 (figures illustrative). Nearly all misses sit on one boundary — "too expensive" against "didn't use it enough" — a definition to split, not a training problem. The coefficient said nothing about whether price was the right lens on churn.

## Application

- Fix the unit of analysis first; much apparent disagreement is two people segmenting differently, which is a separate computation.
- Double-code 10-25% sampled across the corpus, coders working blind. Once they have discussed a unit, agreement on it measures the conversation.
- Treat a low score as a diagnostic, not a verdict: fix the codebook, recode, re-measure.
- Report which coefficient, how many coders, what share was double-coded, and the prevalences.

## Limitations

- **It measures consistency, not correctness.** Nothing in the statistic asks whether the codebook describes the data.
- **It is sensitive to prevalence and marginals.** Coefficients from different corpora do not compare cleanly, even for coder pairs of equal skill.
- **It buys little on small interpretive work.** On eight interviews and one analyst, the overhead exceeds what the number supports.

## Common misconceptions

- **"High IRR means the codebook is right."** It means the codebook is applied *consistently*. Two raters can reliably apply a scheme that misses the phenomenon entirely, agreeing perfectly on categories no user would recognise. Reliability is a precondition for a defensible claim, never evidence for one.
- **"Kappa above 0.8 is the standard."** The familiar bands — slight, fair, moderate, substantial, almost perfect — are conventions attributed to Landis and Koch, who offered them as arbitrary benchmarks, not derived cutoffs.
- **"IRR makes qualitative analysis objective."** O'Connor and Joffe: it improves the systematicity, communicability and transparency of coding. It does not convert interpretation into measurement.

## Tips

Keep the disagreement matrix, not just the coefficient: it names which codes got confused, usually one or two adjacent definitions — a codebook fix, not retraining.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Computing the coefficients and confusion matrix, and checking your choice fits the data's shape.
- Stress-testing a codebook before coders touch it: which edge cases do two definitions both admit?

**Where it fails**
- Standing in as the second coder. A model coding the same corpus twice agrees with itself to a degree that looks like excellent reliability and measures only its own determinism; a human-versus-model kappa reports how model-like that human is, not whether the codebook holds.
