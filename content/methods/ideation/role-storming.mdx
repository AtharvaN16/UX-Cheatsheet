## What is it

Participants generate ideas from an assumed persona's point of view — a skeptical power-user, a tough competitor, a regulator — rather than their own, answering "what would this person think or build" instead of speaking as themselves.

## Purpose

People self-censor ideas that feel too obvious, too risky, or too far from their own professional voice to say as themselves. Assigning a persona creates psychological distance from ownership of the idea — it's the persona's idea, not yours — which measurably loosens what people are willing to say, and it forces perspectives (the harshest critic, the most demanding customer) into the room that a same-voice brainstorm tends to skip entirely.

## When to use

- The group keeps circling the same few ideas and needs a genuinely different vantage point, not just more of the same kind of idea
- A key stakeholder perspective (a regulator, a competitor, a specific user segment) is influencing the decision but isn't in the room
- Participants are self-conscious enough that a straight brainstorm is producing safe, obvious ideas
- You want to stress-test an existing idea by having someone argue it from an adversarial or skeptical persona

## How to do it

1. Run a normal brainstorming round first — this surfaces the obvious ideas and gets them out of the way before role-storming pushes past them.
2. As a group, list specific personas relevant to the problem: a skeptical power-user, your toughest competitor, a first-time user, a regulator, a customer who churned.
3. Assign one persona per person (or have the group work through personas together, one at a time).
4. Have each person generate ideas explicitly speaking as that persona — "as the competitor, I'd build X because Y" — for a timed round.
5. Rotate through 2-4 personas total; more than that and the exercise loses momentum.
6. Collect the ideas and move to a normal convergent step, noting which persona each idea came from — that context often matters when deciding what to prioritize.

## Common mistakes

- **Skipping the initial normal brainstorm** — role-storming is meant to go beyond the obvious ideas a group would generate anyway; without that baseline, it's hard to tell whether the persona actually added anything.
- **Choosing vague personas** — "a user" isn't specific enough to generate a distinct perspective; "a user who's tried and abandoned three competitor products" is.
- **Confusing this with bodystorming** — role-storming is a mental perspective shift done seated, in discussion; it doesn't involve physically acting out a scenario in a space the way bodystorming does. Don't ask people to physically perform the persona unless that's a deliberate choice to run bodystorming instead.
- **Letting one persona dominate every round** — rotating personas is what surfaces different kinds of ideas; sticking with one favorite (usually "skeptical competitor") narrows the exercise back down.

## Tips

Pick at least one persona who would actively hate the current direction — the ideas generated defending against or from that adversarial viewpoint are often more useful than ideas from a sympathetic persona.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Drafting a first list of candidate personas relevant to a problem, for a human to pick from and sharpen — especially useful for surfacing a stakeholder perspective the group hadn't thought to include.

**Where it fails**
- Having a model generate the "as this persona" ideas instead of a person performing the role. The exercise's psychological mechanism — a real person feeling licensed to say something because they're speaking "as" someone else — doesn't exist for a model, which has no ownership to distance itself from in the first place; its output is a persona-flavored idea list, not a role-storming session.
