## What is it

Each participant writes ideas down individually and in silence, then passes their sheet to someone else who reads it and adds to it — repeating over several timed rounds so ideas compound across the group without anyone speaking or being interrupted.

## Purpose

Brainwriting exists specifically to fix brainstorming's two best-documented failure modes: production blocking (only one person can talk at a time, so most of the group is idle or forgetting their own idea while waiting) and evaluation apprehension (fear of sounding dumb out loud). Writing in silence removes both — everyone generates simultaneously, and no one has to say a half-formed idea in front of the room.

## When to use

- The group includes people who are quiet, junior, or otherwise unlikely to speak up in an open brainstorm
- You've already tried open brainstorming and suspect a few voices dominated the output
- You want everyone's independent first idea captured before anyone else's opinion can anchor it
- The group is remote or async-friendly and a written, rotating format fits the tooling better than live discussion

## How to do it

1. Frame a single, clear problem prompt everyone writes against.
2. Give each participant paper or a card and have them write down a fixed number of ideas — 3 is standard — in a short, timed round, typically 5 minutes.
3. At the end of the round, rotate: everyone passes their sheet to the next person, who reads what's there and either builds on those ideas or adds new ones of their own.
4. Repeat for several rounds — the classic "6-3-5" implementation runs 6 people through exactly 6 rounds of 3 ideas each in 5 minutes, yielding up to 108 ideas in half an hour, but the structure scales down cleanly to 4 or 5 people if that's what you have.
5. Collect all sheets at the end and move into a separate convergent step — cluster, dedupe, and prioritize once generation has fully stopped.

## Common mistakes

- **Allowing any verbal discussion during the writing rounds** — the entire point is silence; even brief clarifying comments reintroduce the anchoring and evaluation apprehension the method exists to remove.
- **Skipping the rotation** — having people write ideas without ever reading and building on someone else's sheet turns this into parallel individual brainstorming, which is fine but isn't brainwriting; the compounding effect comes specifically from reading what's already there.
- **Using a prompt that's too narrow for the 3-ideas-per-round structure** — if participants run dry after round two, the prompt was probably too specific; widen it.
- **Not budgeting time to converge afterward** — 100+ ideas on paper with no follow-up prioritization step just becomes a pile no one acts on.

## Tips

Run it digitally with a shared doc or board when the group is more than 6-7 people or is distributed — the rotation mechanic (each person's ideas visible to the next reader) is what matters, not physical paper.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Digitizing and deduplicating the raw output across all rounds into a single sorted list before the convergent step.

**Where it fails**
- Using a model to simulate the "building on someone else's idea" rotation step. The value of brainwriting's rotation is a specific human reading another specific human's half-formed thought and extending it in a way that person wouldn't have — a model completing the pattern statistically isn't the same mechanism, even if the output looks similar on the page.
