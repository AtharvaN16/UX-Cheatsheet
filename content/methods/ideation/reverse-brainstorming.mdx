## What is it

Ask "how would we cause or worsen this problem" instead of "how do we solve it," generate answers freely, then flip each one into its opposite to surface a solution you likely wouldn't have reached by asking the direct question first.

## Purpose

Some problems resist direct ideation because the direct question is either too abstract to grab onto or too familiar for anyone to see past their existing assumptions. Asking how to cause or worsen the problem instead reframes it as something concrete and, often, more fun to answer — people who go quiet when asked "how do we improve retention" get specific fast when asked "how would we make people quit immediately." Flipping those answers back over gets you to solutions the direct framing wasn't producing.

## When to use

- The group is stuck — direct ideation has produced only obvious, already-tried ideas
- You suspect there are hidden assumptions or process flaws that direct questioning isn't surfacing
- The problem is abstract enough that "how do we solve it" doesn't give anyone a concrete place to start
- You want to audit an existing product or flow for hidden failure points, not just generate net-new ideas

## How to do it

1. State the problem you actually want to solve in one sentence.
2. Reverse it into a causing or worsening question: "how could we cause this problem?" or "how could we guarantee the opposite of what we want?"
3. Brainstorm freely against the reversed question — normal brainstorming ground rules apply (defer judgment, go for quantity) since this half of the exercise is still open generation.
4. Group the resulting "bad ideas" or causes into themes once generation stops.
5. Flip each theme back into its opposite — a cause becomes a prevention, a way to worsen something becomes a way to improve it.
6. Prioritize the flipped solutions the same way you'd prioritize output from any other ideation method — feasibility, impact, and how directly each one targets a cause the group hadn't previously named.

## Common mistakes

- **Skipping the flip step** — generating causes of the problem and stopping there produces a good diagnostic list but not solutions; the flip from cause to prevention is the part of the method that actually does the work. (This is the exact step Worst Possible Idea deliberately doesn't have — see that method for the different reason it skips it.)
- **Reversing the question too literally** — "how could we cause slow load times" answered with "add more code" flips awkwardly back into "add less code," which isn't an actionable solution; better reversed questions target behavior and process, not just physics.
- **Treating the reversed brainstorm as less serious and rushing it** — the causing/worsening ideas need the same quantity and range as normal brainstorming, or there's nothing substantive to flip.
- **Using it as a substitute for direct ideation on every problem** — it's specifically useful when the group is stuck; running it by default on problems where direct ideation works fine just adds a detour with no payoff.

## Tips

Reverse brainstorming and Worst Possible Idea are often confused because both start by generating "bad" ideas — the difference is the flip: reverse brainstorming is a structured inversion technique that systematically turns each cause into a solution, while Worst Possible Idea has no flip step at all and is really about removing the pressure to sound smart. Pick reverse brainstorming when you want the flip to do real diagnostic work; pick Worst Possible Idea when the goal is just to loosen up a stuck group.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Generating a first pass of "how to cause this problem" answers to seed a human session, especially useful for a facilitator drafting the reversed prompt itself, which is often the hardest part to phrase well.

**Where it fails**
- Doing the flip step for you without domain context. A model can invert a sentence's polarity mechanically, but a genuinely useful flip usually requires knowing which of several plausible preventions actually fits your product's constraints — a model's flipped list can look complete while quietly proposing solutions nobody could ship.
