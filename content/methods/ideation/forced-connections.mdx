## What is it

Introduce a word, image, or object with no relationship to the problem, then deliberately force a connection between it and the problem at hand — using the mental stretch required to link two unrelated things to reach an angle you wouldn't have found by reasoning directly about the problem.

## Purpose

Fixation is the enemy of ideation — once a group has framed a problem one way, everyone's ideas tend to stay near that framing. Forcing a connection to something with zero relationship to the problem breaks that fixation by requiring the brain to build a bridge between two things that don't naturally belong together; the ideas that come out of that bridge-building are reliably farther from the obvious solution space than anything generated by thinking harder about the problem directly.

## When to use

- The group is fixated on a narrow set of solution types and direct ideation keeps circling back to them
- You want ideas from further outside the obvious solution space than brainstorming or brainwriting tend to produce
- You have time for one more divergent pass after a first round has already produced the predictable ideas
- You're comfortable with the process feeling a little arbitrary or silly at first — it works despite that, not because the random input is secretly relevant

## How to do it

1. State the problem clearly, in the same one-sentence form you'd use for any ideation method.
2. Get a genuinely random word, image, or object — a dictionary opened to a random page, a randomly drawn card, an object grabbed without looking. The randomness matters; picking something you already sense a connection to defeats the exercise.
3. List attributes, associations, and properties of the random stimulus on its own, with no reference to the problem yet — if the word is "umbrella," list: opens/closes, protects, portable, comes in different sizes, associated with rain.
4. Force a connection between each attribute and the problem, one at a time — "how does 'protects' relate to reducing onboarding drop-off?" — and write down whatever comes out, even if it feels strained at first.
5. Push through the initial awkward, forced-feeling connections; the useful ideas tend to show up after the first few obvious, weak links, not in them.
6. Collect the resulting ideas and move into a normal convergent step alongside ideas from any other method used in the session.

## Common mistakes

- **Picking a stimulus that's secretly relevant** — a word like "navigation" for a wayfinding problem isn't random, and using it defeats the mechanism; true randomness is what forces genuinely novel angles.
- **Giving up after the first forced connection feels weak** — the first few links are usually strained; the technique's value shows up with persistence, not on the first attempt.
- **Skipping the attribute-listing step and jumping straight to ideas** — listing the stimulus's properties first, separately from the problem, is what generates enough raw material to force multiple distinct connections instead of just one.
- **Using it as the only ideation method in a session** — it's a good tool for breaking fixation after a first, more conventional pass has already produced the obvious ideas; it's a poor first step when the group hasn't even explored the direct solution space yet.

## Tips

Keep a running source of random stimuli on hand — a deck of unrelated object cards, a random word generator, a shelf of random objects — so the exercise doesn't stall on the step of finding something sufficiently random.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Generating a genuinely random word or object list on demand when no physical source is on hand.

**Where it fails**
- Having a model force the connection for you. Models are trained to produce plausible-sounding associations, which means their "forced connections" tend to land on the nearest already-known metaphor rather than the strained, effortful link that actually breaks a human's fixation — the model's fluency short-circuits exactly the productive struggle the technique depends on.
