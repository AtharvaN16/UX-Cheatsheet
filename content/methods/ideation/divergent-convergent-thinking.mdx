## What is it

Generating a wide range of possible answers to an open problem (diverging), then narrowing that range down to the strongest few (converging) — done as two separate, sequential modes of thinking, never mixed in the same moment.

## Why it matters

Evaluating an idea the moment it's spoken kills the volume and range that diverging exists to produce — one skeptical "that won't work" in the first five minutes quietly resets everyone's sense of what's safe to say next. Treating divergence and convergence as distinct phases, each with its own rules, is what protects the raw material a later convergent pass actually needs in order to have good options to choose from.

## Key ideas

- **Diverge first, converge second, never both at once**: judgment and generation use incompatible mental postures — running them simultaneously collapses divergence into premature convergence.
- **Diverging optimizes for quantity and range, not quality**: a divergent phase that only produced "good" ideas wasn't divergent enough; the wild, obviously-flawed ideas widen the space the convergent phase gets to choose from.
- **Converging is its own skill, not the absence of diverging**: it needs explicit criteria (feasibility, impact, cost) and a decision method (voting, scoring, discussion) — without one, convergence quietly defaults to whoever is loudest or most senior.
- **The shape repeats at every scale**: a single ideation exercise diverges then converges in an hour; a full product development process does the same thing across months.

## Example

A team facing a churn problem might open a session by generating 40 possible causes and interventions with no discussion of feasibility (diverge), then spend the second half of the same session grouping, discarding, and dot-voting down to the 3 worth prototyping (converge). Running both halves back to back instead would have collapsed the 40 down to whatever the first two people said.

## Application

- **Name the phase out loud**: state explicitly "we're diverging for the next 15 minutes — no critique" before starting, so the ground rule is shared, not assumed.
- **Separate the room from the room**: use a different board, doc, or call for convergence than for divergence, so the switch is visible to everyone.
- **Budget more time for diverging than feels comfortable**: groups habitually converge too early because holding open options feels unproductive — the discomfort is the point.

## Limitations

- **Doesn't tell you *how* to diverge or converge**: it's a shape, not a technique — you still need a specific method (brainstorming, brainwriting, dot-voting) to execute either phase.
- **Two clean phases is an idealization**: real sessions blur at the edges, and enforcing a hard boundary can feel artificial to participants who naturally think in loops.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Divergent thinking is the creative part and convergent thinking is just admin"**: convergence requires its own creativity — choosing criteria, spotting which "bad" idea has a good insight buried in it — and doing it badly wastes everything divergence produced.
- **"More divergent time always produces better ideas"**: past a point, additional divergence just restates the same ideas; a good facilitator watches for that flattening rather than running a fixed clock regardless of what's happening in the room.

## Tips

Watch the group's energy, not just the clock — if idea generation has visibly slowed and people are repeating themselves, that phase is done regardless of how much time is left on it.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Generating a first pass of divergent options fast, as raw material a human still diverges from further — a way to seed a divergent phase, not replace it.
- Clustering and labeling a large pile of raw ideas during convergence, which is exactly the kind of tedious sorting a model handles quickly.

**Where it fails**
- Treating a model's fluent, endless idea list as evidence of real range. A model tends to regress toward the statistically likely answer even when asked to be wild — its "divergent" output is narrower than it looks, and a group that outsources divergence to it inherits that narrowness without noticing.
