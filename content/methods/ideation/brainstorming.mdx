## What is it

A group generates as many ideas as possible on a prompt out loud, under four ground rules — defer judgment, go for quantity, welcome wild ideas, combine and build on others' contributions — with evaluation deliberately withheld until generation stops.

## Purpose

Brainstorming exists to produce a large, varied pool of raw ideas fast, by removing the social cost of saying something dumb — the ground rules aren't etiquette, they're the mechanism. Deferred judgment specifically is what lets people say the idea they'd normally self-censor, and some fraction of self-censored ideas turn out to be the useful ones.

## When to use

- You need a fast, low-cost first pass on a problem before investing in more structured ideation
- The group is new to working together and a shared, energetic activity helps build trust before deeper collaboration
- The goal is quantity and range over depth — you'll converge and filter later, not now
- Participants are reasonably comfortable speaking up in front of each other (see Common mistakes if they're not)

## How to do it

1. Frame the prompt narrowly enough to be actionable but broadly enough to allow many answers — "how might we reduce onboarding drop-off" beats both "fix onboarding" and "should the third screen say 'Continue' or 'Next'."
2. State the ground rules out loud before starting: defer judgment, go for quantity, welcome wild ideas, combine and build.
3. Set a visible timer — 15-20 minutes is typical — and have someone capture every idea without editing or paraphrasing it into something more "reasonable."
4. Keep the pace fast; silence longer than a few seconds usually means someone is self-editing before speaking, not that ideas have run out.
5. Stop generating and move fully into a separate convergent step — don't let discussion of feasibility creep in before the timer's up.

## Common mistakes

- **Letting evaluation creep in during generation** — "we tried that" or "that won't work" the moment it's said resets the room's sense of what's safe to say next, and quietly kills the rest of the session's range.
- **Assuming the ground rules alone solve production blocking and evaluation apprehension** — they don't; research on real versus nominal groups found production blocking (only one person can talk at a time) accounts for most of the productivity loss real groups show compared with the same people working alone, no matter how well the ground rules are followed.
- **Treating one loud brainstorm as sufficient evidence of the "best" idea** — the volume of ideas a group produces together is reliably lower than the same headcount working alone and pooling results afterward; brainstorming's real value is energy and shared buy-in, not raw idea count.
- **Running it with a rank imbalance in the room** — a session with a senior stakeholder present produces measurably more conformity and fewer genuinely wild ideas, regardless of what's said about deferring judgment.

## Tips

Run a silent, individual writing round for a minute or two before opening the floor — it gives introverts and lower-status participants a private first idea to build from, which softens (though doesn't eliminate) the loudest-voice-wins dynamic of open brainstorming.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Priming a session with an unfiltered first batch of ideas from a model before humans start, purely to break the blank-page problem, not to replace the group's own generation.
- Capturing and lightly organizing a fast-moving group's shouted-out ideas in real time without slowing the pace down.

**Where it fails**
- Substituting a model-generated list for the group exercise itself. The documented value of live brainstorming is energy, momentum, and shared ownership of the ideas that come out of it — a static list a model produced alone has none of that, even if the ideas on it are individually fine.
