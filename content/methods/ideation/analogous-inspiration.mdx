## What is it

Deliberately studying a different, unrelated context where the same underlying behavior, emotion, or constraint shows up, then extracting the mechanism — not the surface details — and adapting it back to your own design problem.

## Purpose

Breaks a team out of solving a problem only within its own industry's existing patterns. A domain that looks nothing like yours can face the exact same underlying constraint — timing under pressure, trust between strangers, recovering from an error — and studying how it's handled there hands you a mechanism you'd never generate by only looking at your own competitors.

## When to use

- Your team keeps proposing the same handful of solutions because everyone's looking at the same competitor products
- The core of your problem is a behavior or constraint, not a feature — "how do people build trust with a stranger fast," "how do people wait without getting frustrated" — that plausibly shows up elsewhere
- You're early in a discovery process and want to widen the solution space before converging
- You have time to actually go observe or research another context, not just imagine it from a desk

## How to do it

1. Name the specific behavior, emotion, or constraint at the core of your problem — be precise. "People feel anxious about an irreversible decision" is searchable; "improve checkout" is not.
2. Brainstorm contexts, unrelated to your industry, where that same element shows up prominently — an ER waiting room for anxious waiting, a casino for irreversible high-stakes decisions, an orchestra for tight timing coordination among strangers.
3. Pick one or two of the most promising analogous contexts and go observe them directly, or interview people who operate in them — don't just imagine what they're like.
4. While observing, note the mechanism the analogous context uses to handle the shared element, not its surface details — you're not trying to make your product look like a casino.
5. Bring the mechanism back and ask explicitly: what would this look like translated into our context, stripped of the domain it came from?
6. Sketch or prototype the translated mechanism and test whether it actually addresses your original problem, not just whether it makes for a fun analogy.

## Common mistakes

- **Copying the surface, not the mechanism** — borrowing an airline boarding pass's visual style because "airlines are analogous to fast checkout," without understanding why boarding passes actually work (clear sequencing, an unambiguous next step), transfers nothing useful.
- **Picking an analogous domain that's too close** — comparing your app to a directly competing app isn't analogous inspiration, it's competitive research; the value comes from the domain being structurally similar but surface-different.
- **Skipping the observation step** — reasoning about what a casino or ER "probably does" from imagination, instead of actually watching it, produces a guess dressed up as a finding.
- **Never translating back** — treating the analogous domain as the interesting output, instead of a means to generate a concrete, testable idea for your actual product.

## Tips

Keep the framing question narrow and behavioral, not broad and topical — "how do people recover quickly after making a mistake in public" finds better analogues than "how do other apps handle errors."

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Brainstorming a wide list of candidate analogous domains for a given behavior or constraint, as a starting list for the team to select from and actually go observe
- Drafting interview or observation guides tailored to a chosen analogous context

**Where it fails**
- Substituting a model's plausible-sounding description of "how casinos handle high-stakes decisions" for an actual visit or interview. The entire value of the method is grounding the mechanism in something real; a model's generic, averaged-out description of a domain it never observed is exactly the kind of surface-level guess this method exists to avoid.
