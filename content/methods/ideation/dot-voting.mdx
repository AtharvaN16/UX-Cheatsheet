## What is it

Each participant gets a fixed, equal number of dot marks and places them on their preferred options from an already-generated set on a board or wall; tallying where the dots land shows what the group collectively favors.

## Purpose

Converges a group on a shortlist fast, without a long discussion, by making everyone's preference visible and countable at once. It's the tool for narrowing a large set of already-generated ideas down to a workable few — not for judging which of those ideas is actually the best strategic bet.

## When to use

- You have a large set of ideas or options already generated (from a brainstorm, design studio, or lotus blossom session) and need to narrow it fast
- Group discussion is dragging or a few loud voices are dominating, and you want every participant's preference counted equally
- You need a visible, shared artifact of what the group favored, not just a verbal consensus that's hard to point back to later
- Speed matters more than rigor — you'll apply a more rigorous prioritization method afterward if the stakes are high

## How to do it

1. Post the full set of generated ideas or options somewhere visible — a wall, a whiteboard, a shared digital board.
2. Give every participant the same fixed number of dots — commonly a number roughly a third to a quarter of the total options on the board.
3. Set the rule explicitly: can a participant put multiple dots on one option, or only one dot per option? Decide before voting starts, not after.
4. Have everyone vote at the same time and, ideally, silently — voting in view of others invites people to follow the room instead of voting their real preference.
5. Tally the dots and visually cluster where they landed — the pattern typically shows a few clear favorites and a long tail.
6. Discuss the results briefly to catch split votes or an option that clearly needed more context before people could evaluate it fairly.

## Common mistakes

- **Letting people campaign before voting** — a participant lobbying for their own idea right before the vote skews the count toward persuasion, not genuine preference.
- **Voting in full view, one at a time** — later voters anchor on where dots have already landed, collapsing the independence that makes the tally meaningful.
- **Treating the winning dot count as a final decision** — dot voting measures what a group finds appealing in the moment, not feasibility, cost, or strategic fit; an idea with fewer dots can still be the right call once those are weighed.
- **Using it on a set of ideas nobody has actually understood yet** — voting works on ideas people can evaluate at a glance; if an option needs real explanation first, a quick vote just measures who explained their idea best out loud.

## Tips

Use color-coded dots when you want two signals at once — one color for "I love this," another for "I have concerns about this" — so the tally surfaces both enthusiasm and risk instead of just raw popularity.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Tallying and clustering votes quickly from a photo of a physical dot-voting board, when the session ran in person and needs a clean digital record
- Summarizing which options got split votes or a surprising pattern, as a prompt for the follow-up discussion

**Where it fails**
- Any use that predicts or substitutes for the actual group's preference. Dot voting's entire value is that it's a real signal from the specific people in the room; there's no way to model that ahead of time, and no reason to — the method is cheap and fast enough that skipping straight to a guess defeats the point of running it.
