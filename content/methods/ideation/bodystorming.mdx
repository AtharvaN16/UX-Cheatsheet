## What is it

Physically acting out a scenario in a real or improvised physical space — moving through it, handling props, playing out roles — to generate design ideas and surface friction points that talking or sketching alone would never reveal.

## Purpose

Surfaces problems that only show up when a body is actually moving through a scenario — spatial awkwardness, timing, who's in whose way — which reasoning through a flow on a whiteboard reliably misses. It trades the efficiency of discussion for the specificity of physically being there.

## When to use

- The problem has a physical or spatial dimension — queueing, wayfinding, handoffs between people, device placement — that's hard to reason about abstractly
- You're designing a service or environment, not only a screen, and want to feel where the friction actually is
- The team has gone stale talking through a flow in the abstract and needs a different mode to unstick ideas
- You can access or improvise the real setting, or something close enough — a store floor, a waiting room, a hospital bay

## How to do it

1. Pick a specific scenario and roles — who's the user, who else is in the scene (staff, bystanders), what the goal is.
2. Set up the space, real or improvised — move furniture, use cardboard or other props to stand in for equipment or devices that don't exist yet.
3. Warm up first. People feel self-conscious acting things out; a short, unrelated physical warm-up loosens the group before the real scenario starts.
4. Act out the scenario in real time, including the awkward parts — don't stop to discuss mid-scene, just note friction as it happens.
5. Introduce a complication partway through (a queue forms, a device fails) to see how the scenario actually holds up under stress.
6. Debrief immediately after each run: what felt wrong, what surprised the group, what to change — then run it again with the change.

## Common mistakes

- **Discussing instead of doing** — the moment the group stops moving and starts debating "well, what if the user did X," the exercise has quietly turned back into a regular meeting.
- **Skipping the warm-up** — self-consciousness is real, and a group that hasn't loosened up will under-act the scenario, missing the friction the whole method exists to surface.
- **Treating one run as the answer** — a single pass shows one path through the scenario; edge cases and failure modes only show up once someone introduces a complication or runs it again differently.
- **Using it for problems with no physical dimension** — bodystorming a pure information-architecture decision or a backend logic question wastes the room's energy on the wrong kind of problem.

## Tips

Assign one person to just observe and take notes or photos instead of acting — the people inside the scene are too busy performing it to notice what an outside eye catches.
