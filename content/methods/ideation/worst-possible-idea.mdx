## What is it

Deliberately generate the worst, most absurd, or most unworkable ideas you can for a problem — on purpose, with no requirement to flip or fix them afterward — to remove the pressure to sound smart and loosen up a group that's stuck.

## Purpose

Groups often go quiet or produce only safe, obvious ideas because everyone is quietly managing how smart they sound. Explicitly asking for terrible ideas removes that pressure entirely — there's no way to look bad by suggesting something intentionally bad — and the resulting looseness tends to carry over into whatever comes next. As a side effect, some "bad" ideas turn out to contain a real insight once someone asks why it's bad.

## When to use

- A group is visibly tense, overly polite, or stuck producing only safe ideas
- You need an icebreaker that also produces usable creative material, not just a warm-up exercise
- Participants are new to working together or new to ideation formats and need permission to be wrong
- You want to surface hidden assumptions by examining exactly what makes an idea bad

## How to do it

1. State the real problem, then explicitly invert the ask: "give me the worst possible ideas for solving this — the more absurd, the better."
2. Let the group generate freely for a timed round; encourage genuinely terrible, impractical, or ridiculous answers, not just mediocre ones.
3. Once generation stops, go through the list and name specifically what makes each idea bad — which property, which assumption, which failure mode.
4. Look at the properties that make ideas bad and ask whether their opposite, or some version of the underlying insight, points toward something workable — this step is optional and looser than reverse brainstorming's structured flip, but it's often where the real payoff is.
5. Move into normal ideation on the real problem — the group is now looser and has usually surfaced a few unexpected leads to build on.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating it as purely an icebreaker with no further use** — the bad ideas are often worth a second look; skipping straight past them to "real" brainstorming throws away material that took effort to generate.
- **Confusing it with reverse brainstorming** — this method has no required flip step; it's about psychological safety and loosening up, not a systematic inversion technique. If you want each bad idea methodically turned into a solution, use reverse brainstorming instead.
- **Being too gentle with "bad"** — mildly weak ideas don't create the same permission effect as genuinely absurd ones; push the group further than feels comfortable.
- **Running it with a group that's already comfortable and producing wild ideas** — it's a corrective for a specific blockage (excess caution), not a default opener for every session.

## Tips

Go first yourself with the worst idea you can think of, especially as a facilitator — modeling genuine absurdity, not a half-hearted "bad" idea, sets the bar for how far the group is willing to go.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Generating a large batch of absurd starter ideas quickly, particularly useful when a facilitator needs to model the exercise or get a stuck group moving before their own ideas arrive.

**Where it fails**
- Substituting model-generated "bad ideas" for the group's own. The psychological benefit is specific to people in the room hearing each other say something ridiculous out loud and feeling the tension drop — a pre-written list a facilitator reads off doesn't create that shared release, even if the ideas themselves are equally absurd.
