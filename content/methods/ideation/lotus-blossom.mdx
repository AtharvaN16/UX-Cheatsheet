## What is it

A radiating 3×3 grid: a core idea sits in the center cell, eight related ideas fill the surrounding cells, and each of those eight becomes the new center of its own 3×3 grid — expanding outward in layers.

## Purpose

Pushes a group past the first, most obvious layer of ideas by forcing them to keep going — each idea generated becomes the seed for eight more, so the structure itself prevents a session from stopping at the first plausible answer the way open brainstorming often does.

## When to use

- A brainstorm has produced a shallow set of obvious ideas and needs a structured push to go deeper
- You want breadth (many angles on a theme) and depth (each angle explored further) in the same exercise
- The core topic is broad enough to support 8 distinct related themes — a narrow, single-answer question won't fill the grid
- You have a group willing to work through a visual, somewhat mechanical structure rather than free-associate

## How to do it

1. Write the core problem or theme in the center cell of a 3×3 grid.
2. Fill the 8 surrounding cells with 8 distinct ideas or themes directly related to the core — different angles, not variations on the same angle.
3. Take each of those 8 ideas and make it the center of its own new 3×3 grid.
4. Fill the 8 cells surrounding each new center with further related ideas — now one layer more specific or more developed than the theme that seeded it.
5. Repeat outward for as many layers as useful — a full second layer around all 8 first-layer themes generates up to 64 new ideas.
6. Review the full radiating structure and pull out the ideas — usually from the outer layers — that are specific and actionable enough to develop further.

## Common mistakes

- **Filling cells with near-duplicates** — 8 barely-different phrasings of the same idea defeats the purpose; each of the 8 surrounding cells should be a genuinely distinct angle on the center.
- **Stopping after the first layer** — the technique's real value is in the second radiating layer, where ideas get specific enough to actually act on; stopping at layer one is close to a regular mind map.
- **Starting with too narrow a core topic** — a core question with only two or three plausible related themes leaves cells forced and padded just to fill the grid.
- **Treating every generated idea as equally worth pursuing** — a full grid produces volume, not automatic quality; it still needs a deliberate pass to select the strongest outputs.

## Tips

Do the first layer as a group, then split into pairs or individuals to develop the second layer around each of the 8 themes in parallel — it's faster than doing all 64 cells as one group, and different people naturally take different themes in different directions.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Generating candidate ideas to fill out surrounding cells quickly once a group has set the center theme, as raw material to react to rather than a finished set
- Drafting several complete second-layer expansions in parallel so a small group can review and pick strong threads instead of generating everything by hand

**Where it fails**
- Running the whole grid without any human idea in it. A model filling in every cell from just the center topic tends to produce plausible-sounding but generic themes at every layer — the technique's value comes from a group's specific knowledge feeding each layer, not from mechanically completing a grid shape.
