## What is it

A row-per-item spreadsheet of every piece of content you have: what it is, where it lives, who owns it, when it last changed. An *inventory* records what exists; an *audit* adds the judgment — keep, revise, merge, kill. Do both in one sheet.

## Purpose

Replaces everyone's partial memory with one enumerated list. You cannot card-sort content you haven't listed, or argue about cuts without a row to point at. Veen's claim: whoever builds it becomes the only person who knows how the whole thing fits together.

## When to use

- Before a redesign, replatform or CMS migration, where count and formats set the scope
- Before card-sorting or a taxonomy, to get the item set
- When nobody can say how many pages exist within an order of magnitude
- When content quality is the suspected problem and every complaint is anecdotal

## How to do it

- **Decide the grain first.** Page-level rows suffice for structural work; asset-level rows are needed for migrations. Mixing the two mid-sheet is how an inventory becomes unusable.
- **Fix the columns before collecting.** Veen's set holds: link ID, title, URL, document type, controlled-vocabulary keywords, owner; NN/g adds format, dates and metadata. Add an ROT flag — redundant, outdated, trivial.
- **Crawl, then walk it by hand.** A sitemap.xml or CMS export gets the URLs. Hunt what a crawler can't see: help centre, PDFs, email templates, in-product copy.
- **Add audit columns in a second pass**, once the list is stable: accuracy, traffic, a keep/revise/merge/remove call and its reason. Separating the passes stops people redefining "keep" mid-discovery.
- **Sample-verify 5% of rows, then name an owner and an update trigger**, or the sheet is stale in a quarter.

## Example

A university department site was assumed to hold "maybe 300 pages". A crawl plus a sweep of faculty subdirectories found 2,100 URLs — 640 for courses that no longer ran, 180 near-duplicate admissions pages from four offices. "Redesign the site" became "retire 820, consolidate 180 into 12, card-sort the rest".

## Common mistakes

- **Starting without agreed columns**, so three people fill in "type" three ways and the sheet can't be filtered. Columns are a schema; changing them means re-doing rows.
- **Stopping at the count**, which gives you breadth and nothing about quality. Delivering it after the new IA is drawn fails in reverse: a checklist for a structure it should have informed.
- **Letting the crawler be the whole method.** It finds what's linked and public; orphaned pages, gated content and in-product copy are invisible, and disproportionately broken.
- **Delegating it as a junior task.** The knowledge from walking every page is most of the value, and it leaves with the contractor.

## Limitations

- **A snapshot with a short half-life.** Any site with active publishing diverges from the sheet the day you stop.
- **It tells you what exists, never what's missing.** Gaps surface only against user tasks or a competitor's coverage.
- **Quality columns are judgment, not measurement.** Without a rubric and a calibration pass, two auditors recommend differently on the same page.
- **Traffic biases the cut toward findable content.** A page with no visits may be worthless, or just buried by the architecture you're about to fix.

## Tips

Force one of keep / revise / merge / remove onto every row, with a *reason*. That converts an inventory into a decision document, and the reasons survive the meeting where a stakeholder defends their page.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Normalizing a messy crawl or CMS export into consistent columns.
- Clustering near-duplicate pages and drafting per-page summaries, so a reviewer triages from the sheet rather than 2,000 URLs.

**Where it fails**
- Producing the audit recommendations. A model fills a keep/revise/remove column for every row and it looks like a completed audit — but it cannot know whether a page is current, who depends on it, or which of two duplicates is maintained. A recommendation on a page nobody opened is worse than an empty cell.
