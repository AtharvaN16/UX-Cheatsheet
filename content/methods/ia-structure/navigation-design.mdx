## What is it

The set of navigation systems a product exposes, and which part of the structure each reveals. Rosenfeld, Morville and Arango split them into *embedded* systems inside the hierarchy and *supplemental* ones like search outside it. Designing navigation means populating those slots deliberately instead of accumulating menus.

## Purpose

Structure decides what is findable in principle; navigation decides what is findable in practice. It both moves people and tells them where they are, and Laubheimer finds that second job the most commonly missed on website menus.

## When to use

- You have a validated hierarchy and must decide how much to expose, where
- The product grew a new section and the top level no longer holds it
- Analytics show people reaching content only through search or direct links

## Structure

- **Global navigation**: every screen, top level only — the most constrained and most contested slot on the page.
- **Local navigation**: siblings and children within a section, where deep hierarchies survive or fail since global nav carries one level.
- **Contextual navigation**: inline links between items the hierarchy doesn't connect, honouring a polyhierarchy without duplicating pages.
- **Supplemental navigation**: search, A–Z indexes, the sitemap — routes for people the hierarchy doesn't serve.
- **Utility navigation**: account, cart, language, settings — separate from content, constantly confused with global navigation.
- **Wayfinding cues**: current-state indicators, breadcrumbs, titles — the "you are here" layer that makes the other five interpretable; far cheaper designed in than retrofitted. WCAG 2.4.5 and 2.4.8 apply.

## How to use

- **Start from the site map and the top tasks, not the page template.** Global slots go to tasks that earn them, not departments that ask.
- **Assign every branch to one of the systems above.** Anything in none is reachable only by search — decide that explicitly.
- **Default to visible over hidden.** NN/g measured hidden desktop navigation used by 27% of participants versus 48–50% for visible patterns.
- **Specify keyboard and screen-reader behaviour with the visuals** — a fly-out is an interaction pattern, not a hover effect.
- **Tree-test the structure, then usability-test the navigation** — they fail for different reasons, and one test conflates them.

## Example

A payroll SaaS hid all nine top-level areas behind a desktop hamburger because the mobile app did; support tickets clustered on two features customers never found. A left rail for the five weekly-task areas made the same nine reachable without touching the hierarchy.

## Common mistakes

- **Hover-activated submenus.** They don't exist on touch, fire on accidental pass-through, and are hostile to motor impairment. Click behaves the same everywhere.
- **No current-location indicator.** People can navigate perfectly and still not know where they landed, making every later choice a guess.
- **Treating search as structural cover.** Search helps people who already know what to call the thing — a supplement, not a repair.
- **Rebuilding native menu semantics in divs.** Widgets that look right but carry no roles or focus management are a routine accessibility failure the WAI tutorial exists to prevent.

## Limitations

- **It cannot repair a bad hierarchy.** If categories are wrong or labels unrecognised, every pattern exposes the same wrong structure more attractively.
- **Global navigation matters less than its share of the argument.** Most sessions start deep, from search or a link, so the menu is usually a recovery tool, not the primary path.
- **The typology says nothing about visual weight.** It tells you *which* system a branch belongs in, not what should dominate the page; identical assignments can perform very differently.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Auditing every page template for a missing current-state indicator, breadcrumb or skip link
- Checking menu markup against WCAG criteria and flagging missing roles or focus handling, to confirm in a screen reader

**Where it fails**
- Proposing the navigation structure from a product description. The output is the average nav bar of the training data, reading as your users' mental model when it reflects none. It will also call a custom menu accessible from markup alone, a claim only operating it can settle.
