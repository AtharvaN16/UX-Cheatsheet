## What is it

Participants sort a set of topics into groups that make sense to them, and name those groups in their own words. You watch how people naturally categorize your content instead of imposing a structure on them.

## Purpose

Tells you how people think your content should be organized, before you commit to a navigation, a sitemap, or a set of categories. It replaces internal opinion ("I think this belongs under Settings") with evidence about how outsiders actually group things.

## When to use

- You're building or overhauling an information architecture and don't yet have a structure to test
- You suspect your internal team's mental model of the content doesn't match users'
- You need category labels, not just groupings — an open sort surfaces the words people reach for

## How to do it

1. Pick 40-80 representative content items (fewer if the domain is narrow) and write each on its own card, physical or digital.
2. Decide open, closed, or hybrid. Open: participants create and name their own groups. Closed: they sort into categories you already have. Hybrid gives you both a check and new ideas.
3. Recruit 15-30 participants; more than ~30 rarely changes the pattern, per Spencer.
4. Run individually, remote or in person (a tool like Optimal Workshop's card-sort product, or a physical card deck, both work). Ask participants to think aloud as they sort.
5. Analyze with a similarity matrix or dendrogram to find clusters that recur across participants, not just any one person's idiosyncratic grouping.
6. Turn recurring clusters into a draft structure, using participants' own group names as a starting point for labels.

## Common mistakes

- **Card labels are too vague or too specific** — either produces near-random sorting. Pilot with 2-3 people first.
- **Reading a single participant's sort as "the answer"** — one person's structure is an anecdote. Look for the pattern across the group.
- **Skipping the "why"** — a think-aloud protocol that captures *why* someone grouped two things together is often more useful than the grouping itself.
- **Using your own jargon on the cards** — participants will sort by matching your terminology back to itself rather than by genuine conceptual grouping, which produces a structure that looks validated but isn't. This is the specific trap the NN/g article on terminology matching documents.

## Tips

Run an open sort before you've committed to any category names — a closed sort too early just confirms your own bias. If stakeholders resist spending time on this, a remote unmoderated card sort with 20 participants can be turned around in under a week.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Drafting the initial list of content items from a sitemap or CMS export, so you start from completeness rather than memory
- Clustering open-sort results at scale — an LLM can suggest candidate group names from participants' own labels faster than manual affinity mapping

**Where it produces confident garbage**
- Predicting how users would sort your content instead of running the study. A model's guess about "intuitive" categories reflects general web conventions, not your specific users' mental model — the entire reason to run a card sort is that those two things often disagree.

## Notes
