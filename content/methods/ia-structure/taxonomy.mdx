## What is it

The classification scheme underneath a product's content: approved concepts, their relationships, and the rules for assigning items to them. It lives in a spreadsheet or CMS fields, never on screen — users see the navigation built on it, wearing the words labeling chose.

## Purpose

Gives one durable answer to "where does this belong?" that survives whoever is asking. Without a scheme each item is filed by whoever published it, and findability decays one item at a time.

## When to use

- Items arrive faster than anyone can file them, and the menu cannot keep up
- One concept is described three ways ("plan", "tier", "subscription level"), so filters and search cannot match across them
- You need faceted filtering, or an item belongs in two places and a single tree forces a false choice

## Structure

- **Terms (the controlled vocabulary)**: approved concepts, one preferred form each, variants kept as equivalents; Z39.19 covers how to formulate them.
- **Hierarchical relationships**: broader/narrower links, one organizing principle per level. A term with two parents is a polyhierarchy.
- **Equivalence relationships**: synonyms, abbreviations and legacy names mapped to the preferred term, so search matches words you rejected.
- **Facets**: small independent taxonomies for separate dimensions — light, care level, price band. Users combine them; a hierarchy makes them pick one path.
- **Governance rules**: who may add a term, and when the scheme is reviewed.

## How to use

- Start from the content inventory, not the menu; the menu hides whole classes of item.
- Group it from outside evidence — card-sort clusters, search logs, support-ticket wording — then decide per dimension: hierarchy or facet, where independent variation means facet.
- Write one preferred form per concept, rejected variants as equivalents. Pilot-classify 30 to 50 items with two people separately; disagreement shows where the rules stay ambiguous.
- Hand the groups to labeling, evaluate with tree-testing, record an owner.

## Example

A plant shop filed 900 SKUs in one tree: Indoor > Low light > Ferns, so a Boston fern that also takes bright indirect light never appeared under "Bright". Facets for light, care and height surfaced it under every true combination. Defining that facet was taxonomy; naming it "Light needs" not "Lux tolerance" was labeling.

## Common mistakes

- **Designing from the org chart**: departments become top-level terms, and users do not know your departments.
- **Mixing organizing principles in one level**: an audience ("For students") beside a format ("Videos") makes filing a coin flip, for publisher and searcher alike.
- **Collapsing the scheme into the menu**: navigation cannot show everything a taxonomy carries, so the facets and equivalences search needs get dropped.
- **No equivalence terms, no owner**: people who type a rejected word get zero results and rarely report it, and an ungoverned scheme gains duplicates until the facets stop being trusted.

## Limitations

- **Cannot make a badly named group usable**: a correct grouping behind a vague label still fails, and taxonomy work never surfaces it — labeling and tree-testing do.
- **Facets multiply maintenance**: each must be populated for every item, and a facet at 30% coverage is worse than none — the filter silently hides valid results.
- **No test validates a taxonomy directly**: card sorts judge grouping and naming, tree tests judge the navigation on top; the relationships are only checked by proxy.
- **Any scheme freezes one reading of the content**: it fits today's catalogue, misclassifies the next product line, and costs more to re-facet with every item tagged.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Proposing candidate facets and value lists from a CMS export, and flagging inconsistently described concepts.
- Bulk-tagging content against an approved scheme, with a human checking a sample first.

**Where it fails**
- Generating the scheme from a product description. A model returns a plausible hierarchy reflecting general web convention and your prompt's phrasing, not how your users group your content — none of the evidence a card sort would supply. The mismatch surfaces as low findability in a tree test.
