## What is it

The structural layer of a product: how content and functionality are grouped, named, related, and made findable. It's the skeleton, not the skin — an architecture lives in inventories, hierarchies and relationship models; the menus are the part a user sees.

## Why it matters

Findability complaints arrive dressed as interface problems — "the menu is confusing". Navigation, labels and search can only expose the structure underneath: if two things belong together and the architecture says they don't, no menu design fixes it. Structure is also the most expensive thing to get wrong late: moving a box on paper costs an afternoon, moving it once routes, permissions and indexing depend on it costs a quarter.

## Key ideas

- **Four interlocking systems** (Rosenfeld, Morville and Arango): organization, labeling, navigation, search. Each can be correct while another is broken — a sound hierarchy with wrong labels fails like a bad hierarchy.
- **The structure is invisible; navigation is the tip of it.** NN/g's framing: IA lives in spreadsheets and diagrams, not wireframes. Design navigation first and the structure gets decided by whatever fit the header.
- **Language is the material.** Covert's point: most arguments that look structural are disagreements about what words mean. Until the team settles whether "Reports" and "Insights" name the same thing, every diagram is provisional.

## Example

A B2B analytics product had four top-level sections — Dashboards, Reports, Insights, Explore — all meaning "look at your data", split by whichever team shipped them. A nav redesign changed nothing; a card sort showed users distinguished *saved* from *ad-hoc*, not the four. The fix was one Analyze area faceted that way.

## Application

- **Enumerate before you architect.** You cannot structure content you haven't listed.
- **Name the organizing dimension out loud**, and write down which competing dimension you give up.
- **Get evidence at both ends**: card-sorting to generate groupings, tree-testing to check navigability.
- **Document structure separately from UI** — a site map plus the taxonomy — so it can be reviewed without collapsing into visual design.

## Limitations

- **It cannot resolve a business disagreement about what the product is.** If two divisions can't agree whether they sell one product or three, that fight resurfaces in the navigation.
- **Any hierarchy privileges one dimension.** Product, audience, task or lifecycle — not all four, and users who organize by the one you dropped pay for it. Facets let several coexist, at a maintenance cost nobody budgets.
- **It's unfalsifiable as a whole.** You can test a specific tree with specific tasks; "is our IA good" has no measurement.
- **Search doesn't rescue it.** It ranks against the same labels and groupings the architecture supplies, so bad structure produces bad results, less visibly.

## Common misconceptions

- **"The sitemap is the IA."** A site map is one view of one system — organization — rendered as a diagram. It says nothing about labeling rules, metadata, or relationships that cross the hierarchy.
- **"IA is navigation design."** Cardello's NN/g article exists to draw this distinction: navigation is the visible mechanism, the architecture is the structure it reveals. They're decided at different times, on different evidence.
- **"It's an upfront phase you finish."** Content and features keep arriving. An architecture without a rule for where new things go decays into whatever the last team found convenient.

## Tips

Before proposing a restructure, write the one sentence a user would have to believe for it to work ("people think of these as saved versus one-off"). That sentence is testable with a card sort; a diagram isn't.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Pulling a first-pass content list out of a sitemap.xml, CMS export or crawl.
- Pressure-testing a proposed hierarchy for inconsistency — categories at mixed levels of abstraction, labels used in two senses.

**Where it fails**
- Producing a tidy hierarchy from a content list. It reflects general web conventions and the shape of your existing labels — the two things IA research exists to check — and reads like the output of a card sort, minus any contact with your users' mental models.
