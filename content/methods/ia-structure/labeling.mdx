## What is it

Choosing, testing and maintaining the words that represent content to users: menu items, category headings, breadcrumbs, filter names, link text. A taxonomy decides that two things are siblings; labeling decides what their parent is called on screen.

## Purpose

Labels are the information scent people read to predict what a click will get them. A correct structure behind a vague label performs like a broken one: someone who cannot tell what a category holds skips it. Labeling is also the cheapest repair — changing a word re-files nothing.

## When to use

- A tree test shows a task failing with *high* directness — people went confidently to the wrong place. That is a wording problem, not a grouping problem.
- Search logs show people asking for something by a word that appears nowhere in your interface
- The same thing is called three things across screens, after growth by team or acquisition
- Marketing has proposed branded category names and you need an evidence-based counter-argument

## How to do it

- Inventory every label and where it appears — menu, heading, breadcrumb, filter, title tag. Conflicts between surfaces show up immediately.
- Harvest candidates from users' own language: card-sort group names, search queries, support-ticket phrasing. Not a naming brainstorm.
- Draft two or three variants per group and record why each loser lost; that record stops the argument reopening every quarter. Prefer clarity over cleverness; forced parallelism across a menu is not worth an awkward label.
- Test the shortlist with tree-testing against findability tasks, or a first-click test for a handful.
- Write winners into one label table, rejected synonyms recorded as equivalence terms in the taxonomy so search still matches them.

## Example

A payroll product's settings had two top-level items, "Company" and "Organisation". In a tree test, "change who approves timesheets" failed while directness stayed high: almost everyone clicked "Company" first, and it held billing. Renaming them "Billing & plan" and "People & permissions" fixed the task without moving a page.

## Common mistakes

- **Vague action verbs as category names**: "Explore", "Discover", "Solutions" differentiate nothing, so people who cannot predict the contents skip the item.
- **Shipping internal terminology**: the spec's name becomes the menu label, and it tests fine inside the company because everyone testing knows the word.
- **Relabeling when the structure is broken**: tree-test failures with heavy backtracking mean people are lost in the grouping, and renaming only relocates the confusion. Go back to taxonomy.
- **Labels that exist only in the menu**: one concept named differently in breadcrumb, heading and filter destroys any sense of having arrived.
- **Icon-only labels for anything unconventional**: magnifier and hamburger are learned; a custom glyph with no word beside it is an unlabeled category.

## Limitations

- **Cannot rescue a wrong grouping**: a better word on the wrong parent only makes it more attractive, pushing success rates down rather than up.
- **Label performance is task-dependent**: a label winning across five findability tasks can lose on a sixth nobody wrote; the result is local to your sample.
- **Clarity and brand voice genuinely conflict**: testing quantifies what a branded name costs in findability, but cannot decide whether the brand should pay it.
- **Menu-length labels cannot carry the scheme's precision**: two words cannot express every distinction a taxonomy makes, so resolution is lost however well the label tests.

## Tips

Treat zero-result site-search queries as a standing labeling backlog — each is someone naming the thing in their own words. Add them as equivalence terms before renaming around one query.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Generating label variants to test against each other, and screening a draft menu for vague verbs, jargon and overlap.
- Clustering site-search and support-ticket phrasing to surface the words users reach for.

**Where it fails**
- Picking the winning label. A model's choice reflects general web convention and its own fluency, not your users' vocabulary, and it will justify that choice convincingly enough to read like a tree-test result — indistinguishable from a tested label until findability drops.
