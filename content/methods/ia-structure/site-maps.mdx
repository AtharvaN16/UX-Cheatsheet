## What is it

A diagram of every place content lives and how those places nest — one node per page, parent-child lines between them: the hierarchy made visible, without navigation UI or visual design.

Three things share the name and aren't this: the footer "Site Map" page, the sitemap.xml file crawlers read, and information architecture itself — the whole system of organization, labelling and navigation, of which the map depicts one part.

## Purpose

Turns hierarchy into something a team can argue about in one view. Until it's drawn, depth and breadth get decided one page at a time by whoever ships next, and nobody notices a top task drifting four levels down. It then anchors tree-test tasks, navigation scope and redirects.

## When to use

- You're planning a redesign or migration and need the current structure visible before committing to a new one
- You have card-sort clusters and need a structure from them: a top level, a depth, a home for every orphan
- The team keeps disagreeing about where something belongs, with no shared artifact to point at

## How to do it

- **Start from a crawl, not memory.** The pages nobody remembered are where the problem lives, and the current-state map is what redirects get built from.
- **Set the top level first.** Every node inherits it, so it is the most expensive decision on the map.
- **Place the card-sort clusters, then handle the leftovers.** Orphans and outliers are the real work. Number the nodes so tickets reference the same one.
- **Decide depth branch by branch.** Flat means long menus and blurry category boundaries, deep means more clicks and more wrong turns — see Whitenton on flat versus deep. High-traffic tasks earn shallow placement.
- **Mark cross-links separately from parent-child lines.** A page in two branches is a polyhierarchy; drawing it twice hides the decision instead of recording it.

## Example

A regional insurer's crawl put a sixth of its 380 pages under Customer Service, burying "Start a claim" — the most frequent reason anyone visits — four levels down. Drawn as a hierarchy, that is the first thing you see: promote Claims, retire the dormant Policy Support pages.

## Common mistakes

- **Mirroring the org chart.** Departments feel correct inside the company and arbitrary outside it, because users don't know which team owns what.
- **Drawing the navigation instead of the hierarchy.** A map holding exactly the header items is a picture of the nav bar; most structure sits below what navigation exposes.
- **Building it from opinion and calling it validated.** A workshop map is a hypothesis: card sorting supplies its input, tree testing its evidence.
- **Letting it go stale.** A map that no longer matches the CMS is worse than none, because people still trust it.

## Limitations

- **A tree can't express a structure that isn't one.** Faceted browsing, tag systems and search-first products route people laterally; parent-child lines flatter a structure the product lacks.
- **It says nothing about findability.** A coherent hierarchy built on labels nobody recognises still fails, and only a tree test tells you which case you're in.
- **It implies a journey most users don't take.** Sessions typically start deep, from search or a link, not at the root. The sitemap page it often spawns is barely used: 7% of participants turned to one in Nielsen's second study, down from 27%.

## Tips

Keep the map and the content inventory in one artifact, one node per page, so structure and content status (owner, last updated, keep/merge/kill) cannot drift apart.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Turning a crawl or CMS export into a first-pass nested outline, flagging orphans, duplicate titles and deep branches
- Drafting the redirect table between current-state and future-state maps, mechanical and error-prone by hand

**Where it fails**
- Generating the structure from a product description. A model returns a tidy hierarchy that looks like the output of a card sort and a tree test, but it reflects general web conventions, not your users' grouping — and those disagreements are exactly what is worth mapping.
