## What is it

A systematic check of a product against accessibility standards — usually WCAG — combining automated scanning tools with manual testing: navigating with only a keyboard, using a screen reader, checking color contrast and zoom/reflow behavior.

## Purpose

Finds barriers that block people using assistive technology or with disabilities from using the product at all, not just barriers that make it merely inconvenient. Automated tools alone catch roughly a third of WCAG issues; manual testing is what catches the rest — things like whether a screen reader announces a form error in a way that's actually useful, or whether focus order actually follows visual reading order.

## When to use

- You're shipping to a legal or contractual standard (WCAG 2.1/2.2 AA is the common bar) and need to know your conformance status
- You suspect keyboard or screen reader users are hitting specific barriers and want to locate them precisely
- You're auditing before a redesign ships, to establish a baseline of known issues
- Automated scanning already flagged issues and you need manual confirmation of severity and real-world impact

## How to do it

1. Pick the target conformance level explicitly — WCAG 2.2 Level AA is the standard default for most legal and contractual requirements.
2. Run automated scanning tools (axe, WAVE, Lighthouse) first, as a fast pass to catch the mechanically detectable issues: missing alt text, insufficient contrast, missing form labels.
3. Navigate the entire interface using only a keyboard — no mouse — checking that every interactive element is reachable, has a visible focus indicator, and follows a logical tab order.
4. Navigate with a screen reader (NVDA or JAWS on Windows, VoiceOver on Mac/iOS) to check that content, controls, and state changes are announced in a way that actually makes sense out loud, not just technically present in the markup.
5. Check zoom and reflow behavior at 200%+ browser zoom, and test with a color-blindness simulator for anything conveyed by color alone.
6. Log every finding against its specific WCAG success criterion, with severity and a concrete fix — a flat list of "accessibility issues" without the mapped criterion is much harder for engineering to prioritize and verify against.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating automated scan results as a complete audit** — automated tools catch roughly a third of WCAG issues at best; the rest (meaningful focus order, sensible screen reader announcements, logical heading structure) require a human pass.
- **Testing only with a mouse-and-eyes mental model of "does this look accessible"** — visual inspection alone misses the majority of keyboard and screen reader problems, which live in markup and interaction order, not visual layout.
- **Skipping the mapped success criterion** — a finding like "the search field is hard to use with a screen reader" isn't actionable until it's tied to a specific criterion (e.g., 4.1.2 Name, Role, Value) that tells engineering exactly what's missing.
- **Auditing once and never retesting** — accessibility regresses easily with new features; a single point-in-time audit goes stale fast without periodic re-checks.

## Tips

Test with real assistive technology yourself before hiring specialists for a deeper audit — a basic keyboard-only and screen-reader pass by someone on the team catches a surprising fraction of issues and builds the team's own sense of what "accessible" actually feels like to use.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Mapping raw findings to specific WCAG success criteria and drafting a fix recommendation per issue
- Summarizing automated scan output into a prioritized list, as a starting point before manual verification

**Where it produces confident garbage**
- Judging whether a screen reader experience is actually usable without one being run. A model can check markup against WCAG's technical rules, but whether an announcement sequence *makes sense* to someone listening is a lived, sequential experience — the same gap that makes automated-only audits incomplete applies just as much to an AI-only pass over the markup.
