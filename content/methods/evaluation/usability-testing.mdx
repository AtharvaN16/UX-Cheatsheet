## What is it

You give someone a realistic task and a working prototype or product, then watch them attempt it — where they hesitate, what they misread, what they click that does the wrong thing.

## Purpose

Finds usability problems before they ship, by watching real behavior instead of asking opinions. People are often poor judges of their own confusion in the moment ("oh, I get it now") but their hesitation, backtracking, and errors are directly observable.

## When to use

- You have something people can actually try — a clickable prototype or a live build
- You need to know whether a specific flow works, not whether the overall concept is liked
- You're choosing between two design directions and want behavioral, not just opinion-based, evidence

## How to do it

1. Write 3-5 realistic task scenarios framed around a goal, not your UI ("you want to send $50 to a friend," not "find the transfer button").
2. Recruit 5 participants per round per distinct user segment — Nielsen and Landauer's model shows five participants surface roughly 85% of usability problems in a single round, and testing more without iterating in between wastes budget on diminishing returns.
3. Run one participant at a time, thinking aloud, with a facilitator who asks what they're trying to do rather than leading them toward the right answer.
4. Record completion (did they succeed?), path (how did they get there?), and moments of visible hesitation or error — not just the end state.
5. Fix the most severe, most frequent problems between rounds. Krug's whole framing is that a small, cheap round done monthly beats one large, expensive round done once.
6. Retest the fixed flow in the next round rather than assuming the fix worked.

## Common mistakes

- **Helping the participant** — the moment a facilitator gives a hint, that task's data is contaminated. Sit with the silence.
- **Testing with only 1-2 participants and generalizing** — one person's confusion might be idiosyncratic; a pattern across 3+ of 5 is a real problem.
- **Confusing "they completed the task" with "it was easy"** — someone can succeed after real struggle. Pair completion with a difficulty measure like [SEQ](/m/seq) to catch this.
- **Running one giant round instead of several small ones** — Krug's core argument: a morning a month, fixing the worst problems each time, beats a single exhaustive study that gets shelved.

## Tips

Recruit participants who match your actual user base's *unfamiliarity* level, not just their demographics — someone who's never seen your product before will surface onboarding problems a power-user tester never will.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Drafting task scenarios from a feature spec, then checking each one for accidental hints about the "correct" path
- Turning session recordings into timestamped summaries of where each participant hesitated, so a team can jump straight to the moment instead of rewatching full sessions

**Where it fails**
- Predicting where users will struggle instead of watching them. Heuristic evaluation and expert review are legitimate methods for this kind of prediction when done by a trained evaluator against known usability principles — but an LLM asked to "guess where users get confused" is pattern-matching on generic UI conventions, not reasoning about your specific design, and will produce plausible-sounding predictions regardless of whether they're true.
