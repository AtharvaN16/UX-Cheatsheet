## What is it

The same task set and metrics are run across two or more competing products — often yours plus one or more competitors — with different participants (or the same participants, counterbalanced) attempting equivalent tasks in each, so results can be compared head to head.

## Purpose

Tells you where your product actually stands relative to alternatives, on the same tasks, rather than in isolation. A usability test of your product alone can tell you it's "hard to complete task X" — comparative testing tells you whether that's actually worse than what users already tolerate elsewhere, which changes how urgently it needs fixing.

## When to use

- You're entering a market with established competitors and need to know where you stand on usability, not just features
- Stakeholders are citing a competitor as "better" without evidence, and you need a fair, structured comparison
- You're deciding which of several existing tools or approaches to standardize on
- You want context for your own benchmark numbers — is a 70% success rate good, or is that just what this task category looks like everywhere?

## How to do it

1. Define tasks that are genuinely equivalent across every product being compared — not tasks that happen to favor one product's specific workflow.
2. Decide on a within-subjects design (same participants try every product, counterbalanced to control for order effects) or between-subjects (different participants per product) based on your timeline and risk of learning effects.
3. Recruit 8-12 participants per product condition — more if the products are likely to be close, since small differences need more data to distinguish from noise.
4. Run identical task instructions and identical metrics (success, time, SEQ) across every product, changing only which product the participant is using.
5. Randomize product order for within-subjects designs to prevent the first product tested from suffering an unfair "still learning the tasks" disadvantage.
6. Report results side by side per task, not just an aggregate score — a product that wins overall but loses badly on one critical task is a different finding than a uniform win.

## Common mistakes

- **Writing tasks that favor your own product's workflow** — comparative testing is only fair, and only useful, if the tasks are genuinely equivalent across products; this is easy to get wrong unintentionally.
- **Ignoring order effects in within-subjects designs** — if everyone tries your product first, they're less practiced on it than on whatever they try second; counterbalance the order.
- **Comparing against an out-of-date competitor version** — competitors ship updates too; confirm you're testing their current release, not a stale account of how it used to work.
- **Reporting only an aggregate "we won" or "we lost"** — task-by-task results usually tell a more nuanced, more actionable story than a single rolled-up score.

## Tips

Recruit participants who have never used any of the products in the comparison, where possible — existing familiarity with one product creates an advantage that has nothing to do with that product's actual usability.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Drafting equivalent task wording across products from each one's actual UI, then checking each version doesn't accidentally hint at its product's specific path
- Aggregating task-by-task results across products into a clear comparison table for a stakeholder readout

**Where it produces confident garbage**
- Predicting how your product would compare to a competitor without running the test. Perceptions of competitor usability are often based on marketing polish or a single team member's own experience, not structured data — a model's guess reflecting general reputation or public sentiment repeats that same unvalidated bias rather than correcting it.
