## What is it

One or a small number of experienced UX practitioners review an interface and give their professional judgment on what's working and what isn't — drawing on broad pattern-recognition from prior projects rather than a fixed checklist or a single defined task.

## Purpose

Gets a fast, experienced read on a design's overall quality and likely problem areas, without the setup cost of recruiting users or the narrower scope of a checklist-based inspection. It's especially good at catching issues a team is too close to the product to see themselves, and at pattern-matching against conventions the reviewer has seen fail elsewhere.

## When to use

- You want a broad, experienced opinion on a design's overall direction, not a narrow check against one task or one heuristic list
- You need input fast — a single senior reviewer can turn around a useful review in hours
- You want an outside perspective specifically because your team has been too close to the product to see it clearly
- You're validating a design direction before committing budget to a fuller evaluation or user study

## How to do it

1. Choose reviewers for genuine relevant expertise — ideally at least one who has never seen the product before, since familiarity is exactly what limits an internal team's view.
2. Give reviewers context: who the users are, what they're trying to do, and any real constraints (technical, business, timeline) that bound what's actually fixable.
3. Have each reviewer go through the interface using realistic tasks, not just aimless browsing, so findings connect to actual user goals.
4. Require a structured write-up per finding: what's wrong, why it matters, and (where possible) what would fix it — not just a list of things the reviewer personally dislikes.
5. If using multiple reviewers, have them work independently first, the same way as a heuristic evaluation, before comparing notes.
6. Rank findings by expected impact and feasibility, and separate "this breaks the task" from "this is a stylistic preference."

## Common mistakes

- **Treating a single reviewer's taste as fact** — expert review is judgment, not measurement; a second opinion or a follow-up study matters more here than in methods with harder data.
- **Choosing reviewers with no relevant background** — a "fresh pair of eyes" without usability expertise gives you an opinion, not an expert review; the value of the method depends on genuine expertise.
- **Skipping context on constraints** — a reviewer who doesn't know what's technically or contractually fixed will flag things the team already knows about and can't change, wasting the review's limited time.
- **Presenting findings as unstructured feedback** — a rambling list of impressions is far less actionable than a ranked, reasoned set of specific problems.

## Tips

Ask the reviewer to separate "I would design this differently" from "users will struggle with this" explicitly in their write-up — the two get conflated constantly, and only the second is usually worth acting on without further validation.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Formatting a reviewer's raw notes or recording into a structured, ranked findings report
- Drafting follow-up questions to ask the reviewer where a finding's reasoning or severity is unclear

**Where it fails**
- Substituting for the expert's judgment itself. The value of an expert review is a specific person's accumulated pattern-recognition from real projects and real failures — a model's "expert-sounding" critique is a plausible-looking simulation of that judgment with none of the track record behind it, and presenting it as an expert review misrepresents what actually backs the opinion.
