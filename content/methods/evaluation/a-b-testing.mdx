## What is it

Two or more versions of a product experience are shown to randomly assigned, statistically comparable groups of real users at the same time, and a predefined metric is measured to see which version performs better.

## Purpose

Gives causal evidence — not just correlation — about which design change actually moves a metric, because random assignment controls for every other variable that might otherwise explain a difference. It answers "does this specific change cause this specific outcome," which no amount of watching users in a lab session can, since a lab study can't isolate a change's effect on real behavior at scale.

## When to use

- You have enough traffic to reach statistical significance on your target metric within a reasonable timeframe
- You have a specific, testable hypothesis about a change's effect on a defined metric — not an open-ended "which do people like better"
- The decision is high-stakes or high-traffic enough that the cost of running the test is justified by the cost of shipping the wrong version to everyone
- You want to validate a design change's real-world impact, not just its usability in a moderated session

## How to do it

1. Write a specific hypothesis: this change will move this metric in this direction, for this reason — not just "let's see what happens."
2. Choose one primary success metric before the test starts. Tracking many metrics and picking whichever moved after the fact is p-hacking, not experimentation.
3. Calculate the sample size needed to detect a meaningful effect at your traffic volume, before launching — this tells you upfront whether the test is even viable in your timeframe.
4. Randomly assign users to control and variant, and run both simultaneously — never sequentially — so external factors (seasonality, a marketing campaign, a holiday) don't confound the comparison.
5. Run for the full predetermined duration and sample size. Stopping early the moment a result looks significant inflates your false-positive rate — this is one of the most common ways A/B tests mislead.
6. Analyze against the pre-registered metric and significance threshold, and report the confidence interval, not just a point estimate or a "winner."

## Common mistakes

- **Peeking and stopping early** — checking results daily and stopping as soon as you see significance dramatically increases the odds of a false positive; decide your sample size and stick to it.
- **Testing too many metrics and cherry-picking the one that moved** — with enough metrics tracked, something will show "significance" by chance alone; define the primary metric in advance.
- **Underpowered tests** — running a test with too little traffic to detect the effect size you actually care about produces an inconclusive result that gets misread as "no difference."
- **Ignoring novelty effects** — a redesign can spike engagement simply because it's new, not because it's better; effects that don't hold up after the novelty fades aren't real wins.

## Tips

Segment your results after the fact only for hypothesis generation, never as the basis for a final decision — finding that a variant "won" only among one browser or region, discovered by digging through subgroups after the main result was flat, is exactly the kind of post-hoc pattern that looks real and usually isn't.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Calculating required sample size and expected test duration from your traffic and baseline conversion rate
- Drafting the hypothesis and pre-registration document, and summarizing results into a clear readout once the test concludes

**Where it fails**
- Predicting which variant will win instead of running the test. A/B testing exists precisely because intuition about user behavior at scale is unreliable — a model's confident prediction about which design "should" perform better carries the same unvalidated-intuition problem the method is designed to eliminate, just from a different source.
