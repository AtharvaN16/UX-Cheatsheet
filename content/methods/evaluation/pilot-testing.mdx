## What is it

A small-scale trial run of a study — usability test, survey, or interview — with 1-3 participants, done specifically to catch problems in the *study design itself* before running it for real.

## Purpose

Catches broken tasks, confusing questions, and bad timing estimates before they cost you a full round of real participants. A pilot session's data usually isn't included in your findings — its job is to find flaws in the instrument, not to produce results.

## When to use

- Before any study involving scarce or costly participants — a broken task discovered on participant 1 of a real round has already cost you that slot
- The protocol is new or has changed meaningfully since it was last run
- You're unsure how long sessions will actually take and need a realistic timing estimate
- Multiple facilitators will run the same protocol and need to calibrate to a consistent script

## How to do it

1. Treat the pilot as a real session in every way except that you tell the participant upfront you're testing the study, not just the product — this frees them to comment on confusing questions or tasks directly.
2. Run 1-3 pilot sessions; more than that starts eating into the budget meant for the real round.
3. Time everything — task completion, total session length — against your planned schedule, and adjust if reality diverges.
4. Note every point of confusion in the *instrument itself*: an ambiguous task wording, a leading question, a survey item two ways to interpret.
5. Revise the protocol based on what broke, and — if the study is high-stakes or the changes were significant — run one more pilot on the revised version before going live.
6. Decide explicitly whether pilot data counts toward your real sample. Usually it doesn't, since the protocol it was collected under wasn't the final one.

## Common mistakes

- **Skipping it under time pressure** — this is exactly when problems are most expensive, since a broken task discovered mid-study still costs the participants already run.
- **Silently including pilot data in the final results** — if the protocol changed after the pilot, that data isn't comparable to what follows; decide and document this before running real sessions.
- **Treating the pilot participant like a full study participant** — they should know they're helping test the *study*, which gets you honest feedback about wording and pacing you wouldn't get otherwise.
- **Only checking "does it technically work" and not "does it take the time we budgeted"** — timing surprises compound across a full round of scheduled sessions in a way that's hard to fix after the fact.

## Tips

Recruit pilot participants who are as close as possible to your real target sample, but hold them out of the actual study — a colleague who happens to fit your user profile is a common, low-cost choice.

## Using AI

**Where it helps**
- Reviewing a draft protocol for leading questions, ambiguous task wording, or double-barreled survey items before the pilot even runs
- Summarizing what changed between the pre-pilot and post-pilot protocol versions, for a clean record of what the revision addressed

**Where it fails**
- Predicting whether a task or question will confuse participants instead of actually running the pilot. The entire point of piloting is that authors are too close to their own wording to reliably spot what a genuine outsider will misread — a model's guess about "clarity" has the same blind-spot problem as the author's own read, just phrased more confidently.
